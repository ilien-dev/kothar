# Typography in logos and brand names (wordmarks / logotypes): peer-reviewed evidence on consumer responses

Verification protocol used in these notes

- Every citation below was confirmed against a fetched record (Crossref, OpenAlex, Semantic Scholar, PubMed/Europe PMC, or the publisher abstract page). "Verified: metadata + abstract" means I read the abstract; "Verified: metadata only" means title/authors/journal/DOI were confirmed but no abstract or full text could be read.
- Full texts of the marketing papers (Journal of Marketing, JCR, JMR, JCP, Journal of Retailing) are paywalled and were NOT read. So per-study N, country, test statistics and effect sizes are mostly not available here. Where a number appears, it comes from the abstract. Anything recalled from memory and not confirmed on a fetched page is tagged **UNVERIFIED**.
- Evidence grades: **strong** = replicated or meta-analytic; **moderate** = several studies inside one paper (or two independent papers that converge); **weak** = single study, abstract-only verification, or contested.
- General web search (Bing/DDG) returned no usable scholarly results in this session; discovery ran through OpenAlex title searches, so coverage is good for well-indexed journals and may miss recent or obscure papers.

## 1. Henderson, Giese & Cote (2004): design dimensions, response dimensions, trade-offs

### Takeaway
The paper reduces typeface design to six dimensions (elaborate, harmony, natural, flourish, weight, compressed) and consumer impressions to four (pleasing, engaging, reassuring, prominent), and its central message is that these impressions trade off against each other, so a typeface cannot maximise all four. Which dimension drives which impression could not be verified from the abstract; the directional mapping below is flagged UNVERIFIED.

### Cited Findings
- **F1.1 Six design dimensions, four impression dimensions, trade-offs.** Henderson, P. W., Giese, J. L., & Cote, J. A. (2004). Impression Management Using Typeface Design. *Journal of Marketing, 68*(4), 60–72. DOI 10.1509/jmkg.68.4.60.42736. Verified: metadata + abstract. Abstract (verbatim substance): the article "develops empirically based guidelines to help managers select typefaces that affect strategically valued impressions", discusses "potential trade-offs among the impressions created by typeface (e.g., pleasing, engaging, reassuring, prominent)", and states selection "can be simplified with the use of six underlying design dimensions: elaborate, harmony, natural, flourish, weight, and compressed". Design: large-stimulus-set rating study (design characteristics related to consumer impressions via regression) — design description, sample N, country and coefficients NOT verified (paywalled). Grade: **moderate** (one paper, large stimulus set, highly cited: 464 citations in OpenAlex; dimensions re-used by Grohmann et al. 2013, see F3.1). — [SAGE abstract page](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1509/jmkg.68.4.60.42736); [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1509/jmkg.68.4.60.42736)
  - Candidate rule: decide which ONE or TWO of pleasing / engaging / reassuring / prominent the wordmark must win, then choose the typeface for it; do not expect one face to score high on all four.
  - Measure/check: score a candidate face on the six dimensions — weight (weight class 100–900; stem-width / x-height ratio), compressed (average glyph advance width / x-height; width class), elaborate/flourish (presence of swashes, serifs, ornaments; contour point count), harmony (uniformity of stroke width and proportions = low stroke contrast, consistent glyph widths), natural (organic/handwritten vs geometric construction; curvature share of contours, slant).
- **F1.2 UNVERIFIED directional mapping (from memory, not confirmed on any fetched page).** Recalled: pleasing rises with harmony, natural and flourish and falls with heavy weight/compression; engaging rises with natural and elaborate; reassuring rises with harmony and falls with elaborateness; prominent rises with weight and falls with natural/flourish; hence the trade-off "engaging vs reassuring" (elaborate faces interest but do not reassure) and "prominent vs pleasing" (heavy faces are strong but less liked). Also recalled: 210 typefaces. **All of this is UNVERIFIED** and must be checked against the paper's Table/Figure before being used as a rule. Grade: weak (unverified).
- **F1.3 Earlier connotation studies cited by the paper (reference list seen on the SAGE page):** Rowe (1982, *Information Design Journal* 3(1), 30–37, DOI 10.1075/idj.3.1.03row) found, with a semantic differential on ten display typefaces and lay subjects, that Helvetica (the only sans) "was not perceived as differing significantly from serifed typefaces on most dimensions". Verified: metadata + abstract. Grade: weak (single small study). — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1075/idj.3.1.03row)
  - Candidate rule: do not assume serif vs sans alone carries a strong connotation; overall style matters more.
  - Measure/check: n/a (classification).
- Tannenbaum, Jacobson & Norris (1964), *Journalism Quarterly 41*(1), 65–73, DOI 10.1177/107769906404100108: three groups differing in typographic knowledge rated typefaces; "typography can communicate connotative variations". Verified: metadata + abstract. Grade: weak (old, single study) but it is the first of a long line of consistent results. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/107769906404100108)

### Inferences
- The six dimensions are a workable checklist for describing any wordmark candidate numerically (weight, width, ornament, uniformity, organic-ness), even though the exact regression weights were not verified.
- The repeated finding across 60 years (Tannenbaum 1964, Rowe 1982, Henderson 2004, Grohmann 2013) that typefaces carry consistent connotations is the best-supported claim in this whole area; the specific mappings are less secure.

### Gaps
- Could not read the full text: no N, country, number of typefaces, R² or coefficients. The dimension→impression mapping remains UNVERIFIED.
- No independent replication of the six-factor structure was found outside the same author group (Giese is co-author of Grohmann et al. 2013).

## 2. Typeface semantic associations, memory, appropriateness and choice (Childers & Jass 2002; Doyle & Bottomley 2004, 2006, 2009)

### Takeaway
Typefaces carry meaning independent of the words, that meaning transfers to the brand (assimilation), and a font that fits the product roughly doubles choice share in forced-choice tests. Consistency between typeface meaning and the rest of the message also improves memory for claims.

### Cited Findings
- **F2.1 Typeface semantics affect brand perception and memory.** Childers, T. L., & Jass, J. (2002). All Dressed Up With Something to Say: Effects of Typeface Semantic Associations on Brand Perceptions and Consumer Memory. *Journal of Consumer Psychology, 12*(2), 93–106. DOI 10.1207/S15327663JCP1202_03. Verified: metadata + abstract. Abstract: typeface associations affected brand perceptions "across varying levels of involvement" and "influenced the memorability of advertised benefit claims. Memorability was enhanced as the degree of consistency among typeface semantic cues, advertisement visual cues, and advertisement copy claims increased". Design: experiments with print ads (N, country, statistics not verified). Moderator: involvement level (effect present at both, per abstract). Grade: **moderate**. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1207/S15327663JCP1202_03)
  - Candidate rule: make the typeface say the same thing as the name, symbol and claim; consistency is what helps memory, not the typeface alone.
  - Measure/check: rate the wordmark and the brand's target attributes on the same semantic scales (e.g., evaluation/potency/activity) and compare profiles.
- **F2.2 Appropriate font doubles choice.** Doyle, J. R., & Bottomley, P. A. (2004). Font appropriateness and brand choice. *Journal of Business Research, 57*(8), 873–880. DOI 10.1016/S0148-2963(02)00487-3. Verified: metadata + abstract. Abstract: "In a two-alternative forced-choice task, brands, covering 10 different product categories, were chosen twice as frequently when they were in an appropriate font relative to when they were not. This finding was replicated with no diminution of the effect even when brand names were highly connotative. In a subsequent small field study, consumers chose chocolates from a box of chocolates having an appropriate font rather than one having an inappropriate font on 75% of occasions." Sample: N not verified; authors are UK-based (country of sample not verified). Boundary: forced choice between otherwise identical options — a best case for the effect; the field study is described as small. Grade: **moderate** (replicated within paper; lab + small field). — [ScienceDirect abstract](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0148296302004873)
  - Candidate rule: test the wordmark's font for category appropriateness before anything else; an inappropriate font is a measurable handicap.
  - Measure/check: appropriateness rating (1–7) of the font for the product category from 20–30 target users; forced-choice test against an alternative.
- **F2.3 What "appropriate" means: congruity on potency and activity, plus a main effect of the font itself.** Doyle, J. R., & Bottomley, P. A. (2006). Dressed for the Occasion: Font-Product Congruity in the Perception of Logotype. *Journal of Consumer Psychology, 16*(2), 112–123. DOI 10.1207/s15327663jcp1602_2. Verified: metadata + abstract. Abstract: uses Osgood's evaluation, potency, activity (EPA) to measure fonts and product categories; appropriateness "partly depend[s] on the consistency between the connotative meaning of the font and that of the product along dimensions of potency and activity. However, its perceived appropriateness also depends on the connotative meaning of the font per se, independent of the type of product"; differences also appear "when participants choose a company to call on the basis of ads similar to those found in the Yellow Pages"; the effect of font meaning "is similar to the effect of the meaning they assign to the product's name itself". Grade: **moderate**. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15327663jcp1602_2)
  - Candidate rule: match the font's potency (strong/heavy vs delicate) and activity (dynamic vs calm) to the product; some fonts are simply rated as better across products (evaluation), so avoid low-evaluation fonts regardless of fit.
  - Measure/check: potency proxies = weight class, stroke width, width; activity proxies = slant angle, irregularity, script construction. Confirm with EPA ratings.
- **F2.4 Meaning transfers by assimilation.** Doyle, J. R., & Bottomley, P. A. (2009; online 2008). The massage in the medium: Transfer of connotative meaning from typeface to names and products. *Applied Cognitive Psychology, 23*(3), 396–409. DOI 10.1002/acp.1468. Verified: metadata + abstract. Abstract: "Three experiments demonstrate that people who are asked to evaluate names and products are affected by transfer of meaning from the typeface... The meaning of the word is pulled towards the meaning of the typeface (assimilation), rather than pushed away from it (contrast)." Grade: **moderate** (three experiments, one lab). — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1002/acp.1468)
  - Candidate rule: expect the brand to be read as more like its typeface, never as a contrast to it.
  - Measure/check: before/after ratings of the bare name vs the set wordmark.
- **F2.5 Congruence across visual elements raises credibility and price expectations.** van Rompay, T. J. L., & Pruyn, A. T. H. (2011). When Visual Product Features Speak the Same Language: Effects of Shape-Typeface Congruence on Brand Perception and Price Expectations. *Journal of Product Innovation Management, 28*(4), 599–610. DOI 10.1111/j.1540-5885.2011.00828.x. Verified: metadata + abstract. Two studies with a fictitious bottled-water brand (shape × typeface, luxury vs casual connotation); "overall positive effects of meaning congruence on perceptions of brand credibility and price expectations"; mechanism proposed: processing fluency. Authors are Dutch (sample country not verified). Grade: **moderate**. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1540-5885.2011.00828.x)
  - Candidate rule: symbol and wordmark should connote the same thing (e.g., both luxurious or both casual).
  - Measure/check: rate symbol and wordmark separately on the same scales; flag large gaps.
- **F2.6 Symbol–typeface congruence and positive emotion.** Salgado-Montejo, A., Velasco, C., Olier, J. S., Alvarado, J., & Spence, C. (2014). Love for logos: Evaluating the congruency between brand symbols and typefaces and their relation to emotional words. *Journal of Brand Management, 21*(7–8), 635–649. DOI 10.1057/bm.2014.29. Verified: metadata + abstract. Design: nine real brand logos split into symbol and typeface; N = 80 (half rated logotype, half symbol) on shape-symbolism scales, plus choice among 20 emotions. Result: brands whose components had more similar scores "presented more positive as compared with negative emotions". Correlational, nine logos, real brands (familiarity confound). Grade: **weak**. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1057/bm.2014.29)
  - Candidate rule: keep the roundness/angularity of the symbol and of the letterforms aligned.
  - Measure/check: share of curved vs straight segments and corner radii in both parts.
- **F2.7 Typeface can signal origin when it matches the text's meaning.** Celhay, F., Boysselle, J., & Cohen, J. (2015; online 2014). Food packages and communication through typeface design: The exoticism of exotypes. *Food Quality and Preference, 39*, 167–175. DOI 10.1016/j.foodqual.2014.07.009. Verified: metadata + abstract. One experiment, N = 1718 questionnaire respondents, six "exotypes"; they "are able to communicate a specific exotic origin, especially when the connoted meaning of the typeface is congruent with the denoted meaning of the text". Grade: **weak–moderate** (single large study). — [ScienceDirect abstract](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0950329314001566)
  - Candidate rule: culturally coded letterforms work as origin cues only when the name agrees with them.
  - Measure/check: n/a (qualitative style class).
- **F2.8 Destination logos.** Li, F. S., & Ma, J. (2023). The effectiveness of the destination logo: Congruity effect between logo typeface and destination stereotypes. *Tourism Management, 98*, 104772. DOI 10.1016/j.tourman.2023.104772. Verified: metadata only (title states a congruity effect). Grade: weak (not read). — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tourman.2023.104772)

### Inferences
- Congruence is the most replicated mechanism in the typography–brand literature: it appears in at least five independent papers above (JCP 2002, JBR 2004, JCP 2006, JPIM 2011, FQP 2015) with different stimuli and outcomes (memory, choice, credibility, price expectation). As a class of effect I would grade it **strong in direction**, although no meta-analysis was found and effect sizes are not comparable.
- The "twice as frequently" figure comes from a forced choice between two versions of the same brand; in a real market with other differences the effect will be smaller.

### Gaps
- No N, country or test statistics verified for Childers & Jass 2002 and Doyle & Bottomley 2004/2006/2009 (paywalled).
- No meta-analysis of font–product congruity was found.

## 3. Type font characteristics and brand personality (Grohmann, Giese & Parkman 2013) and brand gender

### Takeaway
Using the Henderson dimensions, Grohmann et al. show that font characteristics move all five Aaker personality dimensions, with naturalness, harmony and flourish doing most of the work and naturalness the key driver for new brand names; colour acts independently of the font. A follow-up shows fonts also set perceived brand masculinity/femininity even against a gendered name.

### Cited Findings
- **F3.1** Grohmann, B., Giese, J. L., & Parkman, I. D. (2013; online 2012). Using type font characteristics to communicate brand personality of new brands. *Journal of Brand Management, 20*(5), 389–403. DOI 10.1057/bm.2012.23. Verified: metadata + abstract. Three experiments. Study 1: font characteristics "(naturalness, elaborate, harmony, flourish and weight) influence brand personality perceptions (excitement, sincerity, sophistication, competence and ruggedness). Type font naturalness, harmony and flourish emerge as the most important type font characteristics evoking higher levels of most personality dimensions." Study 2 (new brand names): "naturalness in type font designs is a key driver". Study 3: "the influence of type font color on brand personality perceptions is independent of the impact of the type font itself". N and coefficients not verified; first author is at Concordia University, Montreal (sample country not verified). Boundary: new/unfamiliar brands. Grade: **moderate**. — [Springer abstract page](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1057/bm.2012.23)
  - Candidate rule: for a new brand, more natural (organic, less geometric), harmonious and moderately flourished letterforms raise most personality dimensions at once; choose colour separately, it does not interact with the font.
  - Measure/check: naturalness (curvature share, slant, stroke modulation resembling a hand), harmony (stroke-width uniformity, consistent proportions), flourish (serif/swash presence), weight class.
- **F3.2 Fonts set brand gender.** Grohmann, B. (2016; online 2014). Communicating brand gender through type fonts. *Journal of Marketing Communications, 22*(4), 403–418. DOI 10.1080/13527266.2014.918050. Verified: metadata + abstract. Font effects on brand masculinity/femininity appeared for names in isolation (Study 1), on signage (2a) and on product labels (2b, 3); they "persisted in the presence of a competing brand gender cue (i.e., brand name with gender associations), and type font and brand name influenced brand gender perceptions independently"; Study 4: fonts influence likelihood to recommend. Which font features map to which gender is not stated in the abstract. Grade: **moderate**. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/13527266.2014.918050)
  - Candidate rule: the font is an independent gender cue; check it does not contradict the intended positioning.
  - Measure/check: masculinity/femininity ratings of the set wordmark; feature proxies (weight, angularity, slant) are plausible but not confirmed by the abstract.
- **F3.3 Serif vs sans in brand logos (recent, single paper).** Zhang, M., Teng, L., Xie, C., Wang, X., & Foti, L. (2025). Serif or sans serif typefaces? The effects of typefaces on consumers' perceptions of activity and potency of brand logos. *European Journal of Marketing, 59*(4), 879–922. DOI 10.1108/EJM-06-2023-0497. Verified: metadata + abstract (truncated). Five studies; Studies 1a/1b: "consumers perceive serif logos as more active but less potent"; moderators: design freedom and brand value; downstream effect on brand attitude. Grade: **weak–moderate** (multi-study, single paper, not yet replicated; contrast with Rowe 1982, F1.3, who found Helvetica not different from serif faces on most dimensions). — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1108/EJM-06-2023-0497)
  - Candidate rule: sans serif reads as stronger/more solid, serif as livelier — treat as a tendency, not a law.
  - Measure/check: serif presence; serif length as % of cap height (Arditi & Cho used 0%, 5%, 10%).
- **F3.4 Serifs and credibility of text (not logos).** Kaspar, K., Wehlitz, T., von Knobelsdorff, S., Wulf, T., & von Saldern, M. A. O. (2015). A matter of font type: The effect of serifs on the evaluation of scientific abstracts. *International Journal of Psychology, 50*(5), 372–378. DOI 10.1002/ijop.12160. Verified: metadata + first part of abstract (the result sentence was cut off in my record, so the direction is NOT reported here). Grade: weak. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1002/ijop.12160)
- **F3.5 Serif vs sans on an e-commerce site: no effect on trust or usability.** Vecino, S., Mehtali, J., de Andrés Suárez, J., González Rodríguez, M., & Fernández-Lanvin, D. (2022). How does serif vs sans serif typeface impact the usability of e-commerce websites? *PeerJ Computer Science, 8*, e1139. DOI 10.7717/peerj-cs.1139. Verified: metadata + abstract. N = 246 volunteers, same font family with and without serifs; measured task time, reading speed, comprehension, trust, loyalty, appearance, satisfaction. "only gender determines user preferences. Females tend to prefer the serif version". Grade: **weak–moderate** (one study, decent N, null result). — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.7717/peerj-cs.1139)
  - Candidate rule: do not expect serif/sans alone to change trust.

### Inferences
- Serif vs sans-serif is the weakest of the classic dimensions: the legibility literature finds essentially no difference (section 7), the trust/usability study finds none, and the personality evidence (F3.3) is one recent paper against an older null. Design decisions on serif presence are better justified by congruence (section 2) than by a claimed universal meaning.

### Gaps
- Abstracts do not give which fonts/features signal masculinity vs femininity (Grohmann 2016) or the effect sizes in Grohmann et al. 2013.
- Lieven et al. (2015, *European Journal of Marketing*) on brand design and brand gender was recalled but not fetched: UNVERIFIED, not used.

## 4. Uppercase vs lowercase

### Takeaway
Four independent papers converge: all-caps wordmarks read as stronger, more authoritative, more competent, more masculine and more premium; lowercase reads as friendlier, warmer, closer and more feminine. On pure legibility, capitals are the more legible case at small sizes because they are physically bigger.

### Cited Findings
- **F4.1 Friendliness vs authority.** Xu, X., Chen, R., & Liu, M. W. (2017). The effects of uppercase and lowercase wordmarks on brand perceptions. *Marketing Letters, 28*(3), 449–460. DOI 10.1007/s11002-016-9415-0. Verified: metadata + abstract. Two studies: "consumers feel closer to lowercase wordmarks, which increase perceptions of brand friendliness compared with the uppercase wordmarks... consumers perceive a higher level of strength from uppercase wordmarks, resulting in an increased perception of brand authority. Additionally... this lettering case effect is mitigated when the wordmark design is complex versus when it is simple." N not verified; authors at Chinese and Canadian institutions (sample country not verified). Grade: **moderate** as a single paper; **strong in direction** once combined with F4.2–F4.4. — [Springer abstract page](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11002-016-9415-0)
  - Candidate rule: all caps for authority/strength, lowercase for friendliness/closeness; the effect weakens when the wordmark is already visually complex.
  - Measure/check: case of the string (all upper / all lower / mixed); complexity (number of contour points, ornaments).
- **F4.2 Case and gender; congruity drives attitude.** Wen, N., & Lurie, N. H. (2018). The Case for Compatibility: Product Attitudes and Purchase Intentions for Upper versus Lowercase Brand Names. *Journal of Retailing, 94*(4), 393–407. DOI 10.1016/j.jretai.2018.10.002. Verified: metadata + abstract. Five studies. Studies 1–2: "lowercase brand names are associated with feminine characteristics while uppercase brand names are associated with masculine characteristics". Studies 3–5: congruity between case and the gender of consumption benefits increases product evaluations and purchase intentions; Study 4: moderated by consumer's biological sex, mechanism = processing fluency; Study 5: "brand case, rather than letter shape, drives these results". Grade: **moderate**. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jretai.2018.10.002)
  - Candidate rule: case has no main effect on liking; it helps only when it matches the product's gendered benefit.
  - Measure/check: case; masculinity/femininity rating of the benefit.
- **F4.3 Competence vs warmth, with a cultural moderator.** Teng, L., Xie, C., Liu, T., Wang, F., & Foti, L. (2021). The effects of uppercase vs. lowercase letters on consumers' perceptions and brand attitudes. *Journal of Business Research, 136*, 164–175. DOI 10.1016/j.jbusres.2021.07.013. Verified: metadata + abstract. Three studies: uppercase → more competent, lowercase → more warmth; mechanism = psychological distance (far vs close); moderated by power distance belief "at the individual and national levels". Grade: **moderate**. — [ScienceDirect abstract](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0148296321004938)
  - Candidate rule: uppercase = competent and distant; lowercase = warm and near. Strength varies with the audience's acceptance of hierarchy.
- **F4.4 Uppercase premium effect.** Yu, Y., Zhou, X., Wang, L., & Wang, Q. (2022; online 2021). Uppercase Premium Effect: The Role of Brand Letter Case in Brand Premiumness. *Journal of Retailing, 98*(2), 335–355. DOI 10.1016/j.jretai.2021.03.002. Verified: metadata + abstract. "Eight experiments, including one eye-tracking experiment, reveal that consumers perceive brands that use all uppercase letters as more premium than those that use all lowercase letters." Mechanism: perceived conspicuousness, moderated by the product's social visibility. Reversal: "for consumers who prefer subtle signals... because these consumers are likely to perceive a conspicuous uppercase brand as gaudy." Choice effect only for status-motivated consumers. Grade: **moderate** (eight experiments, one paper). — [ScienceDirect abstract](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0022435921000233)
  - Candidate rule: all caps to look premium, unless the audience values quiet, inconspicuous signals.
- **F4.5 Legibility: capitals win at small sizes.** Arditi, A., & Cho, J. (2007). Letter case and text legibility in normal and low vision. *Vision Research, 47*(19), 2499–2505. DOI 10.1016/j.visres.2007.06.010. Verified: metadata + abstract (PubMed 17675131). Single unaltered font; all-upper, all-lower, mixed; size thresholds for words and random strings, reading speed; normal and visually impaired participants. "Lower-case thresholds were roughly 0.1 log unit higher than upper. Reading speeds were higher for upper- than for mixed-case text at sizes twice acuity size; at larger sizes, the upper-case advantage disappeared." N not in abstract. Grade: **moderate**; converges with F4.6 and F4.7. — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17675131/)
  - Candidate rule: at the smallest sizes (favicon, 16 px) an all-caps wordmark stays legible about 0.1 log unit (roughly 25%) smaller than the same string in lowercase at the same point size; the advantage vanishes once size is comfortable.
  - Measure/check: cap height in px vs x-height in px at the target render size.
- **F4.6** Pušnik, N., Možina, K., & Podlesek, A. (2016). Effect of typeface, letter case and position on recognition of short words presented on-screen. *Behaviour & Information Technology, 35*(6), 442–451. DOI 10.1080/0144929X.2016.1158318. Verified: metadata + abstract. Minimum time to recognise three-letter bold words on screen: "Upper case letters were recognised faster compared to sentence and lower case letters"; Georgia more legible than Verdana, Calibri, Trebuchet, Swiss 721. Grade: weak–moderate (single study, short words — close to the wordmark case). — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/0144929X.2016.1158318)
- **F4.7** Sheedy, J. E., Subbaram, M. V., Zimmerman, A. B., & Hayes, J. R. (2005). Text Legibility and the Letter Superiority Effect. *Human Factors, 47*(4), 797–815. DOI 10.1518/001872005775570998. Verified: metadata + abstract. Threshold-size method, 6 fonts × 3 smoothing modes × 4 sizes × 10 pixel heights × 4 stroke widths. "Individual lowercase letters were 10% to 20% more legible than lowercase words"; word shape "plays a smaller role, if any" at threshold; Verdana and Arial most legible, Times New Roman and Franklin least; "Increased stroke width (bold) improved threshold legibility but only at the thinnest width tested." Grade: **moderate**. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1518/001872005775570998)
  - Candidate rule: at threshold sizes legibility is carried by individual letters, not by "word shape", so the old argument that lowercase is more legible because of its outline does not hold at logo sizes.
- **F4.8 Brand names are stored with their case and font.** Labusch, M., Duñabeitia, J. A., & Perea, M. (2024). Visual word identification beyond common words: The role of font and letter case in brand names. *Memory & Cognition, 52*(7), 1673–1686. DOI 10.3758/s13421-024-01570-3. Verified: metadata + abstract (truncated: "Both experiments showed longer resp[onse times]..." for modified logos). Two experiments comparing original vs font- or case-modified logos. Grade: weak–moderate. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13421-024-01570-3)
  - Candidate rule: once a brand is known, changing its case or font slows recognition; treat both as part of the identity.
- **F4.9 First letter matters most for identity.** Pathak, A., Velasco, C., & Calvert, G. A. (2019). Identifying counterfeit brand logos: on the importance of the first and last letters of a logotype. *European Journal of Marketing, 53*(10), 2109–2125. DOI 10.1108/EJM-09-2017-0586. Verified: metadata + abstract. Seven brand logos, two experiments: participants were "faster and more accurate in identifying a counterfeit logo when the first letter (vs last letter) of a logotype was manipulated". Grade: weak–moderate. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1108/EJM-09-2017-0586)
  - Candidate rule: put the distinctive design effort in the first letter; it is what people check.

### Inferences
- The case effect is the best-replicated semantic effect of a single typographic variable in this review: four papers from different author teams and journals (2017–2022), all in the same direction (upper = strength/competence/authority/masculine/premium; lower = friendly/warm/close/feminine). Graded **strong in direction**; magnitude unknown (no meta-analysis, effect sizes not read).
- Semantics and legibility point the same way at very small sizes (caps are both more legible and more "authoritative"), so a friendly lowercase wordmark needs a larger x-height to stay legible at 16 px.
- Several of these teams are China-based and Teng et al. find a power-distance moderator, so the size of the effect in low-power-distance Western audiences is less certain.

### Gaps
- No per-study N/effect sizes read. Mixed/title case is under-studied relative to all-upper vs all-lower.

## 5. Handwritten / script typefaces (Schroll, Schnurr & Grewal 2018 and follow-ups)

### Takeaway
Handwritten-looking type signals human presence and raises emotional attachment and evaluations for hedonic, warmth- or care-related products, and the effect has been conceptually replicated in several domains. It reverses for functional products, fades for brands people are already attached to, and can backfire in a product-harm crisis.

### Cited Findings
- **F5.1** Schroll, R., Schnurr, B., & Grewal, D. (2018). Humanizing Products with Handwritten Typefaces. *Journal of Consumer Research, 45*(3), 648–672 (volume/issue/start page confirmed from the OUP URL; end page UNVERIFIED). DOI 10.1093/jcr/ucy014. Verified: metadata + abstract. "Across several laboratory and field studies... handwritten typefaces create perceptions of human presence, which lead to more favorable product evaluations (and behavior) by enhancing the emotional attachment between the consumer and the product. Finally, the effects reverse when the products are functionally positioned or functional in nature." Authors at University of Innsbruck (Austria) and Babson College (USA); sample N/country not verified. Grade: **moderate** (multi-study incl. field) rising to **strong in direction** with F5.2–F5.5. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1093/jcr/ucy014)
  - Candidate rule: use handwritten/script lettering for hedonic, artisanal or care-related offers from little-known brands; avoid it for functional/technical products.
  - Measure/check: construction class (script/handwritten vs typeset); irregularity of baseline, stroke width and glyph repetition (do repeated letters differ?); connected strokes.

- The handwritten effect is among the better-replicated ones (JCR lab + field, then at least three conceptual replications in other journals), but always as an interaction with product type or message (hedonic/warm vs functional/competent). Without that fit there is no reliable main effect.
- For small-size wordmarks there is a cost: script and display faces are perceived as less legible, so a script wordmark needs a larger minimum size or a simplified small-size variant.

### Gaps
- Effect sizes and Ns for Schroll et al. not read. No direct test of handwritten wordmarks at favicon sizes was found.

## 6. Letter spacing, weight, slant, roundness, width; taste, gender, luxury inferences

### Takeaway
Tight letter spacing in text logos signals safety (JCR, five studies incl. field and archival); round letterforms are liked more, read as easier, signal sweetness and suit hedonic products (replicated cross-culturally); heavy vs light weight signals heaviness/unhealthiness; right slant signals forward motion and urgency but only helps promotional messages. Evidence on width (condensed/extended) and on weight-as-luxury in brand perception is thin.

### Cited Findings
- **F6.1 Interstitial space → safety.** Gupta, T., & Hagtvedt, H. (2021). Safe Together, Vulnerable Apart: How Interstitial Space in Text Logos Impacts Brand Attitudes in Tight versus Loose Cultures. *Journal of Consumer Research, 48*(3), 474–491. DOI 10.1093/jcr/ucab006 (erratum: *JCR 49*(1), 175, DOI 10.1093/jcr/ucab054). Verified: metadata + abstract. "interstitial space in textual brand logos—that is, spacious (vs. compact) arrangement of letters—unfavorably influences brand attitude by reducing product safety perceptions. When potential threats are salient, the effect tends to occur within tight (but not loose) cultures... When threats are not salient, the effect appears to occur across cultures. ### Inferences
- Roundness is the dimension with the most consistent semantic evidence (several labs, three countries, lab and field), but the field study shows it moves stated preference more than actual perception or choice.
- Weight as a luxury cue ("thin = luxury") was NOT found in any verified peer-reviewed study in this session; the verified evidence is weight → physical heaviness → healthiness (Karnal et al.) and case → premium (Yu et al.). "Thin = luxury" should be treated as a designer convention, not a finding.
- Slant → speed is supported only indirectly (right slant evokes forward motion in promotions). I found no verified study on slanted brand names and perceived speed of the brand or product.
- Gupta & Hagtvedt's spacing result fits the legibility literature at the tight end only partly: compact is safer semantically, but below standard spacing letters crowd (section 7). The usable window is "compact but not touching".

### Gaps
- No verified study isolating condensed/extended width on brand impressions (only Henderson's "compressed" dimension, mapping unverified).
- No verified study of bold vs thin on luxury or strength of the brand; "perceived strength" evidence comes via uppercase (Xu et al.) rather than weight.
- No verified study of italic wordmarks and perceived speed.

## 7. Legibility science transferable to wordmarks at 16–32 px

### Takeaway
At small sizes, legibility is governed by x-height in visual angle (fluent range about 0.2°–2°), by spacing (crowding, not acuity, is usually the limit), and by stroke design: moderate boldness helps only at small sizes, extremes of weight hurt at every size, high stroke contrast hurts bold faces, wider letters and open apertures help, and serifs make essentially no difference.

### Cited Findings
- **F7.1 Print size: the fluent range is defined on x-height.** Legge, G. E., & Bigelow, C. A. (2011). Does Print Size Matter for Reading? A Review of Findings from Vision Science and Typography. *Journal of Vision, 11*(5), 8. DOI 10.1167/11.5.8. Verified: metadata + abstract. Review. "The fluent range extends over a factor of 10 in angular print size (x-height) from approximately 0.2° to 2°. Assuming a standard reading distance of 40 cm (16 inches), the corresponding physical x-heights are 1.4 mm (4 points) and 14 mm (40 points)." Grade: **strong** (review of a large psychophysics literature). — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1167/11.5.8)
  - Candidate rule: size a wordmark by its x-height, not its nominal font size; keep rendered x-height at or above 0.2° of visual angle at the intended viewing distance.
  - Measure/check: x-height ratio (x-height / em, or / cap height) from font metrics (OS/2 sxHeight / unitsPerEm) × rendered font size in px.
- **F7.2 Crowding: spacing, not size, limits recognition.** Pelli, D. G., & Tillman, K. A. (2008). The uncrowded window of object recognition. *Nature Neuroscience, 11*(10), 1129–1135 (pages UNVERIFIED). DOI 10.1038/nn.2187. Verified: metadata + abstract. Review: "vision is usually limited by object spacing rather than size"; Bouma law: "critical spacing at the visual field is proportional to object distance from fixation"; the effect "is weaker between dissimilar objects". Grade: **strong**. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1038/nn.2187). And Pelli, D. G., Tillman, K. A., Freeman, J., Su, M., Berger, T. D., & Majaj, N. J. (2007). Crowding and eccentricity determine reading rate. *Journal of Vision, 7*(2), 20. DOI 10.1167/7.2.20. Verified: metadata + abstract: "Well-corrected fluent observers reading ordinary text with adequate light are limited by letter spacing (crowding), not size (acuity)", provided contrast is at least four times threshold. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1167/7.2.20). Rule-of-thumb caveat: Coates, D. R., Ludowici, C. J. H., & Chung, S. T. L. (2021). The generality of the critical spacing for crowded optotypes: From Bouma to the 21st century. *Journal of Vision, 21*(11), 18. DOI 10.1167/jov.21.11.18 — Bouma's law is "the rule of thumb stating that flankers within a distance of about one half of the target eccentricity will induce crowding", and the authors examine factors that modulate it. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1167/jov.21.11.18)
  - Candidate rule: a logo seen in the periphery (browser tab, app grid, shelf) is recognised only if its letters are far enough apart relative to how far it is from fixation; a wordmark that is fine when looked at directly can be unreadable at a glance.
  - Measure/check: centre-to-centre letter spacing in px; test recognition while fixating elsewhere.
- **F7.3 Standard spacing is near-optimal; both tighter and looser hurt.** Chung, S. T. L. (2002). The effect of letter spacing on reading speed in central and peripheral vision. *Investigative Ophthalmology & Visual Science, 43*(4), 1270–1276. No DOI in record; PMID 11923275. Verified: metadata + abstract. Six normal observers, RSVP, Courier, spacings 0.5×–2× standard, at fovea, 5° and 10°: "reading speed increased with letter spacing, up to a critical letter spacing, and then either remained constant... or decreased slightly". — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11923275/). Yu, D., Cheung, S.-H., Legge, G. E., & Chung, S. T. L. (2007). Effect of letter spacing on visual span and reading speed. *Journal of Vision, 7*(2), 2. DOI 10.1167/7.2.2. Verified: metadata + abstract: reading speed "peak[s] near the standard letter spacing for text and decreas[es] for both smaller and larger spacings"; mediated by visual span. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1167/7.2.2). Chung, S. T. L. (2012). Dependence of reading speed on letter spacing in central vision loss. *Optometry and Vision Science, 89*(9), 1288–1298. DOI 10.1097/OPX.0b013e318264c9dd: 14 observers; optimal spacing averaged 0.95 ± 0.06× standard. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1097/OPX.0b013e318264c9dd). Korinth, S. P., Gerstenberger, K., & Fiebach, C. J. (2020). Wider Letter-Spacing Facilitates Word Processing but Impairs Reading Rates of Fast Readers. *Frontiers in Psychology, 11*, 444. DOI 10.3389/fpsyg.2020.00444: 24 German students, eye tracking; wider spacing shortened fixation durations per word but gave no words-per-minute benefit. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2020.00444). Grade: **strong** (multiple labs, consistent inverted-U).
  - Candidate rule: keep tracking between roughly the font's default and slightly positive; tracking tighter than default costs recognition quickly, very wide tracking costs it slowly. For a single short word (a wordmark), slightly wider spacing speeds single-word processing.
  - Measure/check: tracking as a multiple of the default advance (Chung's range 0.5×–2×; optimum ≈ 1×).
- **F7.4 Boldness: helps only at small sizes, extremes hurt always.** Beier, S., & Oderkerk, C. A. T. (2019). Smaller visual angles show greater benefit of letter boldness than larger visual angles. *Acta Psychologica, 199*, 102904. DOI 10.1016/j.actpsy.2019.102904. Verified: metadata + abstract (PMID 31421483). Two single-letter experiments: "fonts of thinner letter strokes and of extreme boldness decreased recognition for all tested font sizes, and... there was a positive effect of boldness at small visual angles which did not occur at large visual angles". — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.actpsy.2019.102904). Bernard, J.-B., Kumar, G., Junge, J., & Chung, S. T. L. (2013). The effect of letter-stroke boldness on reading speed in central and peripheral vision. *Vision Research, 84*, 33–42. DOI 10.1016/j.visres.2013.03.005. Verified: metadata + abstract (PMID 23523572). Courier at six boldness levels (0.27, 0.72, 1, 1.48, 1.89, 3.04× standard stroke width): "At the fovea, reading speed was invariant for the middle four levels of boldness, but dropped by 23.3% for the least and the most bold text"; at 10° eccentricity it dropped 21.5% at 1.89× and 51% at 3.04×. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.visres.2013.03.005). Sheedy et al. 2005 (F4.7): bold improved threshold legibility "only at the thinnest width tested". Grade: **strong** (three independent studies agree).
  - Candidate rule: for 16–32 px use regular to semibold; avoid hairline/thin and ultra-black weights. A small-size variant may be slightly bolder than the display version.
  - Measure/check: stem width / x-height; weight class roughly 400–700; rendered stem ≥ 1 device px at the smallest size.
- **F7.5 High stroke contrast hurts bold fonts.** Beier, S., & Oderkerk, C. A. T. (2021). High letter stroke contrast impairs letter recognition of bold fonts. *Applied Ergonomics, 97*, 103499. DOI 10.1016/j.apergo.2021.103499. Verified: metadata + abstract (PMID 34174575). Three purpose-built bold fonts (no, medium, extreme contrast), trigram middle-letter identification at fixation and 2°: "letter recognition impairment for the font with high stroke contrast compared to the fonts with low and medium stroke contrast, while there was no significant difference... between the medium- and low-stroke-contrast fonts". Grade: **moderate**. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.apergo.2021.103499). Related: Minakata, K., & Beier, S. (2022). The dispute about sans serif versus serif fonts: An interaction between the variables of serif and stroke contrast. *Acta Psychologica, 228*, 103623. DOI 10.1016/j.actpsy.2022.103623 — four purpose-built fonts isolating serif and stroke contrast; result sentence not in my record (abstract truncated). — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.actpsy.2022.103623)
  - Candidate rule: Didone-style wordmarks (very thin hairlines against thick stems) need a low-contrast small-size cut; keep thin strokes from disappearing.
  - Measure/check: stroke contrast = thickest stroke / thinnest stroke (e.g., in "o" or "n"); flag ratios that leave the thin stroke under 1 px at target size.
- **F7.6 Open apertures.** Beier, S., & Oderkerk, C. A. T. (2022). Closed letter counters impair recognition. *Applied Ergonomics, 101*, 103709. DOI 10.1016/j.apergo.2022.103709. Verified: metadata + abstract (PMID 35217404). Letters a, c, e, r, s, t, f in three aperture variants, with/without flankers at 2°: "participants had more trouble identifying the letter if it was set in a font variation with closed apertures". Grade: **moderate**. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.apergo.2022.103709)
  - Candidate rule: prefer open apertures in a, c, e, s for small wordmarks.
  - Measure/check: aperture opening / counter width in c, e, a, s.
- **F7.7 Serifs: no meaningful legibility effect.** Arditi, A., & Cho, J. (2005). Serifs and font legibility. *Vision Research, 45*(23), 2926–2933. DOI 10.1016/j.visres.2005.06.013. Verified: metadata + abstract (PMID 16099015). Fonts differing only in serif size (0%, 5%, 10% of cap height): "our data exhibited no difference in legibility between typefaces that differ only in the presence or absence of serifs"; any tiny gain at small sizes is due to "the concomitant increase in spacing". — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16099015/). Moret-Tatay, C., & Perea, M. (2011). Do serifs provide an advantage in the recognition of written words? *Journal of Cognitive Psychology, 23*(5), 619–624. DOI 10.1080/20445911.2011.546781: lexical decision with Lucida serif vs sans; "a small, but sign[ificant]..." effect (abstract truncated; direction not confirmed here). — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/20445911.2011.546781). Perea, M. (2013). Why does the APA recommend the use of serif fonts? *Psicothema, 25*(1), 13–17. DOI 10.7334/psicothema2012.141: eye movements, same family with/without serifs, no differences "except for a minimal effect in the number of progressive saccades". — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.7334/psicothema2012.141). Morris, R. A., Aquilante, K., Yager, D., & Bigelow, C. (2002). Serifs Slow RSVP Reading at Very Small Sizes, but Don't Matter at Larger Sizes. *SID Symposium Digest of Technical Papers, 33*(1), 244–247. DOI 10.1889/1.1830242 (conference digest): sans read "about 20% faster at very small sizes". — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1889/1.1830242). Book-length review: Richardson, J. T. E. (2022). *The Legibility of Serif and Sans Serif Typefaces*. Springer. DOI 10.1007/978-3-030-90984-0 (metadata + one-line abstract only). — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-030-90984-0). Grade: **strong** for "no practical difference at normal sizes"; weak for a sans advantage at very small sizes (one conference paper).
  - Candidate rule: choose serif or sans on meaning and congruence, not legibility; at the very smallest sizes fine serifs may not render and are safer removed or thickened.
  - Measure/check: serif thickness in px at target size.
- **F7.8 Typeface differences at glance.** Dobres, J., Chahine, N., Reimer, B., Gould, D., Mehler, B., & Coughlin, J. F. (2016). Utilising psychophysical techniques to investigate the effects of age, typeface design, size and display polarity on glance legibility. *Ergonomics, 59*(10), 1377–1391. DOI 10.1080/00140139.2015.1137637. Verified: metadata only (author list after the fourth name UNVERIFIED — my record was truncated). — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/00140139.2015.1137637)
- **F7.9 Review of typeface features and legibility.** Bigelow, C. (2019). Typeface features and legibility research. *Vision Research, 165*, 162–172. DOI 10.1016/j.visres.2019.05.003. Verified: metadata + abstract (PMID 31078662). Review; the abstract notes that early research was limited to commercial fonts and that experimental fonts now allow features to be isolated. — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31078662/)
- **F7.10 Pixel height.** Sheedy et al. 2005 (F4.7): "Optimal legibility was attained at 9 pixels (10 points)" in their display conditions; pixel height, font, stroke width and smoothing all had significant main effects; ClearType with Verdana was the most legible condition. Grade: moderate (2005 display technology; the absolute pixel figure may not transfer to high-density screens). — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1518/001872005775570998)

### Inferences
- Conversion of F7.1 to pixels (my arithmetic, not from the paper): 0.2° of visual angle is 1.4 mm at 40 cm and about 2.1 mm at 60 cm. With a CSS reference pixel of about 0.26 mm, that is an x-height of roughly 5 px at 40 cm (phone) and 8 px at 60 cm (desktop). A lowercase wordmark set at 16 px with an x-height ratio of 0.5 has an 8 px x-height, i.e. at the lower edge of the fluent range on a desktop; with an x-height ratio of 0.45 it falls below. An all-caps wordmark at the same size has cap height about 11–12 px, comfortably inside. This is consistent with Arditi & Cho's 0.1 log-unit uppercase advantage.
- Practical small-size checklist derived from the verified findings: x-height ≥ about 8 px at the smallest rendering; weight between regular and semibold; stroke contrast low to medium; thinnest stroke ≥ 1 px; tracking at or slightly above default; regular or wide width, not condensed; open apertures; serifs optional.
- The semantic preference for compact spacing (F6.1) and the legibility penalty for sub-default spacing (F7.3) together argue for a size-dependent tracking: compact at display sizes, loosened for the 16 px version.

### Gaps
- Nearly all legibility studies use running text, single letters or trigrams, not logos; transfer to stylised wordmarks is an inference. The only logo-specific legibility paper found is Ko, Y.-H. (2017), The effects of luminance contrast, colour combinations, font, and search time on brand icon legibility, *Applied Ergonomics, 65*, 33–40, DOI 10.1016/j.apergo.2017.05.015 — metadata only, not read. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.apergo.2017.05.015)
- No verified study of x-height ratio manipulated in isolation for wordmarks. Pušnik, Podlesek & Možina (2016), Typeface comparison — Does the x-height of lower-case letters increased to the size of upper-case letters speed up recognition? *International Journal of Industrial Ergonomics, 54*, 164–169, DOI 10.1016/j.ergon.2016.06.002, addresses it but only metadata was verified. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ergon.2016.06.002)

## 8. Disfluent (hard-to-read) fonts: when they help, when they hurt, replication status

### Takeaway
In consumer research, a hard-to-read font makes everyday goods less attractive but can make special-occasion goods more attractive by signalling uniqueness (one JMR paper, conceptually echoed in Psychology & Marketing). The broader "disfluency helps" literature has largely failed to replicate: pooled data from 17 studies show no benefit on reasoning, and the learning benefit is null in meta-analysis and in preregistered Sans Forgetica tests.

### Cited Findings
- **F8.1 Difficulty helps special-occasion products, hurts everyday ones.** Pocheptsova, A., Labroo, A. A., & Dhar, R. (2010). Making Products Feel Special: When Metacognitive Difficulty Enhances Evaluation. *Journal of Marketing Research, 47*(6), 1059–1069. DOI 10.1509/jmkr.47.6.1059. Verified: metadata + abstract. Four studies. "In the domain of everyday goods, metacognitive difficulty reduces the attractiveness of a product by making it appear unfamiliar. However, in the context of special-occasion products, for which consumers value exclusivity, metacognitive difficulty increases the attractiveness of a product by making it appear unique or uncommon"; depends on the naive theory people hold about the consumption domain. The abstract does not say that the manipulation was font; that it was (hard-to-read font) is from memory and **UNVERIFIED**. No direct replication found. Grade: **weak–moderate** (four studies, one paper, 2010-era methods, no replication located). — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1509/jmkr.47.6.1059)
  - Candidate rule: a slightly demanding, unusual wordmark can signal exclusivity for luxury/special-occasion brands; for everyday brands favour fluent, familiar forms.
  - Measure/check: perceived reading ease (1–7) and recognition time of the wordmark; unusualness relative to category norms.
- **F8.2 Uniqueness vs difficulty compete.** Wu, R., Han, X., & Kardes, F. R. (2021; online 2020). Special fonts: The competing roles of difficulty and uniqueness in consumer inference. *Psychology & Marketing, 38*(1), 86–100. DOI 10.1002/mar.21414. Verified: metadata + abstract. Five experiments. Special fonts are "perceived as more unique and difficult to read than regular fonts"; uniqueness leads to "more favorable initial judgments but a higher likelihood of regret later", difficulty has the opposite effects; uniqueness dominates for categories expected to be unique ("a handmade item or a décor"), difficulty dominates for categories not expected to be unique ("a tax preparation service"). Grade: **moderate**. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1002/mar.21414)
  - Candidate rule: distinctive letterforms pay off in categories where uniqueness is expected; in utilitarian categories the reading cost dominates.
- **F8.3 Hard to read → judged hard to do.** Song, H., & Schwarz, N. (2008). If It's Hard to Read, It's Hard to Do: Processing Fluency Affects Effort Prediction and Motivation. *Psychological Science, 19*(10), 986–988. DOI 10.1111/j.1467-9280.2008.02189.x. Verified: metadata only (PubMed 19000208 has no abstract). Result as stated in the title. Grade: weak (short report; replication status not checked). — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2008.02189.x). Related, metadata only: Oppenheimer, D. M., & Frank, M. C. (2008; online 2007). A rose in any other font would not smell as sweet: Effects of perceptual fluency on categorization. *Cognition, 106*(3), 1178–1194. DOI 10.1016/j.cognition.2007.05.010. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cognition.2007.05.010)
  - Candidate rule: for products that must feel easy (apps, services, instructions), fluent type supports the "easy" promise.
- **F8.4 Original learning claim.** Diemand-Yauman, C., Oppenheimer, D. M., & Vaughan, E. B. (2011). Fortune favors the bold (and the italicized): Effects of disfluency on educational outcomes. *Cognition, 118*(1), 111–115. DOI 10.1016/j.cognition.2010.09.012. Verified: metadata + abstract (PMID 21040910). Study 1 (lab) and Study 2 (high school classrooms): information in hard-to-read fonts was better remembered. Grade: **contested** (see F8.5–F8.8). — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cognition.2010.09.012)
- **F8.5 Failed replication on reasoning (pooled).** Meyer, A., Frederick, S., Burnham, T. C., Guevara Pinto, J. D., Boyer, T. W., Ball, L. J., Pennycook, G., Ackerman, R., Thompson, V. A., & Schuldt, J. P. (2015). Disfluent fonts don't help people solve math problems. *Journal of Experimental Psychology: General, 144*(2), e16–e30. DOI 10.1037/xge0000049. Verified: metadata + abstract (PMID 25844628). "after pooling data from that experiment with 16 attempts to replicate it, we find no effect on solution rates... no evidence of a disfluent font benefit under any conditions... though disfluent fonts slightly increase response times, we find little evidence that they activate analytic reasoning." Grade: **strong** (17 pooled studies). — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1037/xge0000049)
- **F8.6 Meta-analysis: null on learning.** Xie, H., Zhou, Z., & Liu, Q. (2018). Null Effects of Perceptual Disfluency on Learning Outcomes in a Text-Based Educational Context: a Meta-analysis. *Educational Psychology Review, 30*(3), 745–771. DOI 10.1007/s10648-018-9442-x. Verified: metadata only (result stated in title). — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-018-9442-x). Critique: Weissgerber, S. C., Brunmair, M., & Rummer, R. (2021). Null and Void? Errors in Meta-analysis on Perceptual Disfluency and Recommendations to Improve Meta-analytical Reproducibility. *Educational Psychology Review, 33*(3), 1221–1247. DOI 10.1007/s10648-020-09579-1. Verified: metadata + abstract: they "identify some errors and inconsistencies in both the methodological approach and the reported results regarding coding and effect sizes" in Xie et al. (their corrected estimate was not in the portion of abstract read). Grade: **strong-ish for "no reliable benefit"**, with the caveat that the meta-analysis itself has documented errors. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-020-09579-1)
- **F8.7 Boundary conditions for the memory effect.** Yue, C. L., Castel, A. D., & Bjork, R. A. (2013; online 2012). When disfluency is—and is not—a desirable difficulty: The influence of typeface clarity on metacognitive judgments and memory. *Memory & Cognition, 41*(2), 229–241. DOI 10.3758/s13421-012-0255-8. Verified: metadata + abstract (PMID 22976883). Five experiments with blurred vs clear words: judgments of learning were consistently higher for fluent items; recall did not favour disfluent items (no difference with recognition tests or long presentation). Grade: moderate. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13421-012-0255-8)
- **F8.8 Sans Forgetica.** Taylor, A., Sanson, M., Burnell, R., Wade, K. A., & Garry, M. (2020). Disfluent difficulties are not desirable difficulties: the (lack of) effect of Sans Forgetica on memory. *Memory, 28*(7), 850–857. DOI 10.1080/09658211.2020.1758726. Verified: metadata + abstract. Four experiments: Sans Forgetica was rated harder to read than Arial but "led to equivalent memory performance, and sometimes even impaired it". — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/09658211.2020.1758726). Geller, J., Davis, S. D., & Peterson, D. J. (2020). Sans Forgetica is not desirable for learning. *Memory, 28*(8), 957–967. DOI 10.1080/09658211.2020.1797096. Three preregistered experiments, no memory benefit. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/09658211.2020.1797096). Partial exception: Geller, J., & Peterson, D. J. (2021). Is this going to be on the test? Test expectancy moderates the disfluency effect with Sans Forgetica. *Journal of Experimental Psychology: Learning, Memory, and Cognition, 47*(12), 1924–1938. DOI 10.1037/xlm0001042. Three preregistered experiments (N = 231, 232, 232): a benefit "only when there was no expectation of a final test"; "effect sizes [were] moderate" and the authors urge caution. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1037/xlm0001042). Grade: **strong** that a disfluent font does not reliably improve memory.
- **F8.9 Disfluent price fonts are recalled more accurately.** Mead, J. A., & Hardesty, D. M. (2018; online 2017). Price Font Disfluency: Anchoring Effects on Future Price Expectations. *Journal of Retailing, 94*(1), 102–112. DOI 10.1016/j.jretai.2017.09.003. Verified: metadata + abstract. Three experiments: consumers "accurately recall and anchor on disfluent price displays"; mediated by price recall accuracy; stronger for price-conscious and less-involved consumers. Grade: weak–moderate (one paper; runs against F8.5–F8.8, not independently replicated). — [ScienceDirect abstract](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0022435917300738)
- **F8.10 Disfluency from incompleteness** — see Hagtvedt 2011 (F6.11): lower clarity lowers trust; higher interestingness raises perceived innovativeness.

### Inferences
- For brand marks, the defensible claim is narrow: unusual/harder letterforms can signal uniqueness where exclusivity is valued (F8.1, F8.2), and they cost trust and ease elsewhere (F6.11, F8.2, F8.3). The claim "harder-to-read type is remembered better" should NOT be used as a logo rule: its foundations failed large pooled replications and preregistered tests.
- The typeface-roundness result that round faces are "liked more and judged easier to read" (F6.5) and the congruence mechanism via fluency (F2.5, F4.2) both sit on the fluency side: by default, easier processing → more liking.

### Gaps
- No direct or preregistered replication of Pocheptsova et al. (2010) was found in this session; absence of evidence in my search, not proof none exists.
- Song & Schwarz (2008) replication status not checked.

## 9. Which effects are contested, small, or unreplicated? (summary of evidence quality)

### Takeaway
Best supported: typefaces carry consistent connotations; font–product congruence helps; uppercase = strong/premium vs lowercase = friendly; round = pleasant/sweet; handwritten = human (for hedonic goods); legibility rules on spacing, weight extremes and x-height. Weakest or contested: serif vs sans meanings, weight as luxury, slant as speed for brand marks, any memory benefit of hard-to-read type, and the exact Henderson mappings (unverified here).

### Cited Findings
- **Strong (replicated across labs or meta-analytic/pooled):** crowding and spacing limits (Pelli & Tillman 2008 [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1038/nn.2187); Yu et al. 2007 [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1167/7.2.2)); fluent print-size range on x-height (Legge & Bigelow 2011 [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1167/11.5.8)); boldness extremes hurt (Bernard et al. 2013 [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.visres.2013.03.005); Beier & Oderkerk 2019 [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.actpsy.2019.102904)); serifs do not matter for legibility (Arditi & Cho 2005 [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.visres.2005.06.013); Perea 2013 [DOI](https://doi.org/10.7334/psicothema2012.141)); disfluent fonts do not improve reasoning (Meyer et al. 2015, 17 pooled studies [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1037/xge0000049)); round typeface ↔ sweet across three countries (Velasco et al. 2018 [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1037/aca0000120)).
- **Strong in direction, magnitude unknown (several independent multi-study papers, no meta-analysis):** case effects (Xu et al. 2017 [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11002-016-9415-0); Wen & Lurie 2018 [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jretai.2018.10.002); Teng et al. 2021 [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jbusres.2021.07.013); Yu et al. 2022 [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jretai.2021.03.002)); font congruence (Childers & Jass 2002 [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1207/S15327663JCP1202_03); Doyle & Bottomley 2004 [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0148-2963(02)00487-3), 2006 [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15327663jcp1602_2); van Rompay & Pruyn 2011 [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1540-5885.2011.00828.x)); handwritten = human presence with product-type moderation (Schroll et al. 2018 [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1093/jcr/ucy014); Liu et al. 2019 [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jbusres.2019.02.022); Chu et al. 2023 [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1002/mar.21732)).
- **Moderate (multi-study, single paper):** Henderson et al. 2004 dimensions [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1509/jmkg.68.4.60.42736); Grohmann et al. 2013 personality [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1057/bm.2012.23); Grohmann 2016 gender [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/13527266.2014.918050); Gupta & Hagtvedt 2021 spacing–safety [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1093/jcr/ucab006); Hagtvedt 2011 incomplete letterforms [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1509/jmkg.75.4.86); Mead et al. 2020 right slant for promotions [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jretai.2019.10.002); Wang et al. 2020 curvature–hedonic [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1002/mar.21287); stroke contrast and aperture legibility (Beier & Oderkerk 2021 [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.apergo.2021.103499), 2022 [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.apergo.2022.103709)); width legibility (Oderkerk & Beier 2022 [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/00140139.2021.1991001)).
- **Weak, small, moderated or contested:** serif vs sans meaning (Zhang et al. 2025 [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1108/EJM-06-2023-0497) vs Rowe 1982 null [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1075/idj.3.1.03row) and Vecino et al. 2022 null on trust [DOI](https://doi.org/10.7717/peerj-cs.1139)); typeface weight → healthiness only for health-promotion-focused consumers (Karnal et al. 2016 [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.foodqual.2016.04.004)); typeface–taste effects in the field limited to stated preference and to older consumers (Otterbring et al. 2022 [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.foodqual.2021.104376)); difficulty → uniqueness for special-occasion goods (Pocheptsova et al. 2010 [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1509/jmkr.47.6.1059)); disfluency → memory (Diemand-Yauman et al. 2011 [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cognition.2010.09.012), contradicted by Taylor et al. 2020 [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/09658211.2020.1758726), Geller et al. 2020 [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/09658211.2020.1797096), and the Xie et al. 2018 meta-analysis [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-018-9442-x), itself criticised by Weissgerber et al. 2021 [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-020-09579-1)).
- **A font effect that failed in a small test (non-journal, pointer only):** Krueger, A. T. (2016), thesis, OhioLINK ETD: N = 210, job advertisement in Comic Sans vs Times New Roman vs Harrington — "no significant effect of the font in job advertisements on organizational attraction". Not peer-reviewed; shows that font main effects on global evaluations can be nil. — [OpenAlex record search](https://api.openalex.org/works?filter=title.search:Effect%20of%20Font%20in%20Job%20Advertisements%20on%20Organizational%20Attraction)

### Inferences
- A pattern runs through the marketing studies: main effects of a typographic feature on overall liking are rare; what is found reliably is (a) a shift in a specific inference (strength, warmth, safety, sweetness, humanness, premiumness) and (b) a liking/choice gain only when that inference matches the product or the consumer's goal. Rules should therefore be written as "feature X signals Y; use it when Y is wanted", not "feature X is better".
- Almost all marketing evidence uses fictitious or unfamiliar brands, forced exposure, and rating scales; several papers explicitly report that effects weaken for brands people already know or are attached to (Schroll et al. 2018; Grohmann et al. 2013 studies "new brands"). Expect the largest effects at launch and smaller ones for established marks.
- Publication bias is likely: the marketing papers are multi-study packages with uniformly positive abstracts, no preregistration is mentioned in any abstract read, and no meta-analysis of typeface effects on brand responses was found. The only pooled/preregistered evidence in this review (disfluency) overturned the original claims.

### Gaps
- No meta-analysis or large preregistered replication of any typography-and-brand effect was found (searched OpenAlex by title terms: typeface(s), font(s), uppercase, letter case, logotype, serif, slanted fonts, letter spacing, disfluency; general web search engines returned nothing usable).
- A systematic review exists — Yu, M., Abidin, S. Z., Shaari, N., He, C., Shi, L., & Liu, Q. (2024). Effects of brand visual identity on consumer attitude: A systematic literature review. *Environment and Social Psychology, 9*(9). DOI 10.59429/esp.v9i9.3041 (PRISMA, four databases) — but only the opening of its abstract was read and the journal's standing is unknown; treat as a pointer. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.59429/esp.v9i9.3041). Also a book chapter pointer: Velasco, C., & Spence, C. (2019). The Role of Typeface in Packaging Design. In *Multisensory Packaging* (pp. 79–101). Springer. DOI 10.1007/978-3-319-94977-2_4 (metadata only). — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-319-94977-2_4)
- Sample sizes, countries and effect sizes for the paywalled marketing papers remain unverified throughout; anyone turning these notes into quantitative claims must open the full texts.
- Not found/verified in this session: studies on typeface weight and luxury, on italic wordmarks and perceived speed, on condensed vs extended width and brand impressions, on x-height ratio and brand impressions, and on mixed/title case.

# Logo psychology: memory, cross-cultural generalizability, real-world outcomes, replicability

Scope note: colour is out of scope by coordinator instruction; no colour-specific findings are recorded here. Where a cited paper also reports colour results, those are omitted.

Verification convention used throughout:
- **[META+ABS]** = bibliographic metadata (title, authors, year, journal, volume, pages, DOI) AND abstract confirmed against a fetched record (Crossref API, OpenAlex, PubMed, RePEc, publisher page).
- **[META]** = bibliographic metadata confirmed against a fetched record, but the abstract/full text could NOT be fetched; any statement about the findings is marked UNVERIFIED.
- **[FULLTEXT]** = numbers taken from the fetched full text.
- "Abstract says" = stated in the abstract. "Inference" = my reading. Nothing below is cited from memory unless marked UNVERIFIED.
- Grades: strong = replicated / meta-analytic; moderate = multi-study single paper; weak = single study, or contested, or failed replication.

## 1. Memory: how well do people actually remember logos, and what predicts recognition/recall?

### Takeaway
People recognise and name familiar logos almost perfectly but reproduce their details very poorly, and they are overconfident about it; this pattern has been found in three independent samples (US, Romania, Spain) and is the best-replicated finding in this whole note. Evidence on which *design properties* make a logo more memorable is much thinner: it rests mainly on one 1998 correlational study of 195 logos and its cross-national extension, not on replicated experiments.

### Cited Findings
- **Blake, A. B., Nazarian, M., & Castel, A. D. (2015). The Apple of the mind's eye: Everyday attention, metamemory, and reconstructive memory for the Apple logo. *Quarterly Journal of Experimental Psychology*, 68(5), 858-865. DOI 10.1080/17470218.2014.1002798.** [META+ABS] Design: lab study, recall by drawing plus forced-choice recognition, with confidence judgements before/after. Sample: N = 85, UCLA (USA) students. Abstract says: "Only 1 participant out of 85 correctly recalled the Apple logo, and fewer than half of all participants correctly identified the logo"; confidence exceeded performance and overconfidence fell when judgements were made after drawing; no difference between Apple and PC users. Boundary conditions: one logo, one student sample, strict scoring of drawings. Grade for the general claim (poor detail memory + overconfidence): **strong** given the two conceptual replications below; grade for the exact rates: weak (they vary a lot with scoring). — [Crossref record](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1080/17470218.2014.1002798)
- **Iancu, I., & Iancu, B. (2017). Recall and Recognition on Minimalism. A Replication of the Case Study on the Apple Logo. *KOME − An International Journal of Pure Communication Inquiry*, 5(2), 57-70. DOI 10.17646/KOME.2017.24.** [FULLTEXT] Design: self-described "slight replication" of Blake et al. Sample: N = 198 students in Cluj-Napoca, Romania (78 social science, 120 computer science; 119 female; age 18-27). Result: 18.8% (37 respondents) drew the logo correctly under the authors' criteria, versus 1/85 in the original; individual features were remembered well (bite present 96.4%, bite on the right 83.2%, leaf orientation 57.4%). Sex, Apple ownership and attachment to the brand did not significantly predict recall/recognition. Boundary conditions: lenient scoring ("all components correctly considered" even if shape does not overlap), low-tier journal, descriptive statistics only. Grade: weak as a standalone study; it confirms the direction (most people cannot reproduce the logo) but shows the headline rate is scoring-dependent. — [KOME article](https://doi.org/10.17646/kome.2017.24)
- **Mayas, J., Prieto, A., & Montoro, P. R. (2023/2024). Memory and metamemory in everyday settings: Assessing recall, recognition, and naming using car brand logos. *Applied Cognitive Psychology*, 38. DOI 10.1002/acp.4138.** [META+ABS] Design: two studies, recall (drawing) and recognition (Study 1), naming (Study 2). Sample: authors at UNED, Madrid, Spain; N not in abstract (UNVERIFIED). Abstract says: "memory accuracy for logos was modest in the recall and recognition tasks; nevertheless, the participants' accuracy in naming the visually displayed car logos was almost perfect"; overconfidence before recall/recognition, adjusted after the recall task; no overconfidence for naming; "These results replicate the modest visual memory and metacognitive adjustments ... found in previous studies". Grade: moderate (two studies), and it makes the Blake pattern **strong** overall. — [Crossref record](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1002/acp.4138)
- **Signs.com, "Branded in Memory" (no date on page; not peer-reviewed, commercial content marketing).** [FULLTEXT of web page] Method as described: 156 Americans aged 20-70 (mean 34), 10 logos drawn from memory in drawing software (about 31 minutes each, over 1,500 drawings), accuracy rated out of 10 by a panel of five design/marketing professionals. Reported: about 20% drew the Apple logo "almost perfectly", 12% Adidas, 18% Burger King, 16% Domino's, 19% 7-Eleven; frequent intrusions from older logo versions and from wider brand advertising (e.g. 21% added a crown to Burger King). No statistics, no preregistration, no data release, recruitment not described. Use only as an illustration consistent with the peer-reviewed studies. Grade: weak (not peer-reviewed). — [Signs.com page](https://www.signs.com/branded-in-memory/)
- **Henderson, P. W., & Cote, J. A. (1998). Guidelines for Selecting or Modifying Logos. *Journal of Marketing*, 62(2), 14-30. DOI 10.1177/002224299806200202.** [META+ABS] Design: correlational; 195 logos coded on 13 design characteristics, related to consumer responses including correct recognition and false recognition ("false sense of knowing"). Country: USA (authors at Washington State University; rater sample details not in abstract, UNVERIFIED). Abstract says: "High-recognition logos ... should be very natural, very harmonious, and moderately elaborate. Low-investment logos (false sense of knowing and positive affect) should be less natural and very harmonious." Boundary conditions: unfamiliar/foreign logos, logo is the unit of analysis, design scores are judge ratings, no causal manipulation. Grade: **moderate** (large stimulus set, partly reproduced cross-nationally by van der Lans et al. 2009, but never directly replicated and the data are from the 1990s). — [Crossref record](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1177/002224299806200202)
- **van der Lans, R., Cote, J. A., Cole, C. A., Leong, S. M., Smidts, A., Henderson, P. W., Bluemelhuber, C., Bottomley, P. A., Doyle, J. R., Fedorikhin, A., Moorthy, J., Ramaseshan, B., & Schmitt, B. H. (2009). Cross-National Logo Evaluation Analysis: An Individual-Level Approach. *Marketing Science*, 28(5), 968-985. DOI 10.1287/mksc.1080.0462.** [META+ABS] Relevant to memory because "true and false recognition" are among the modelled responses; abstract says responses to the design dimensions "are also relatively consistent" across 10 countries. See section 3. — [Crossref record](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1287/mksc.1080.0462)
- **Janiszewski, C., & Meyvis, T. (2001). Effects of Brand Logo Complexity, Repetition, and Spacing on Processing Fluency and Judgment. *Journal of Consumer Research*, 28(1), 18-32. DOI 10.1086/321945.** [META+ABS] Design: four lab studies. Abstract says stimulus characteristics and presentation factors "interact with repetition to determine the amount of processing fluency associated with a stimulus at various levels of exposure"; the specific direction (which complexity level wins at which exposure level) is NOT stated in the abstract (UNVERIFIED from full text). Grade: moderate. — [OpenAlex record](https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.1086/321945)
- **van Grinsven, B., & Das, E. (2016; online 2014). Logo design in marketing communications: Brand logo complexity moderates exposure effects on brand recognition and brand attitude. *Journal of Marketing Communications*, 22(3), 256-270. DOI 10.1080/13527266.2013.866593.** [META] The title states the moderation; direction and sample are UNVERIFIED (abstract elided by publisher in every source fetched). Grade: weak until read. — [Crossref query result](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1080/13527266.2013.866593)
- **Ward, E., Yang, S., Romaniuk, J., & Beal, V. (2020). Building a unique brand identity: measuring the relative ownership potential of brand identity element types. *Journal of Brand Management*, 27(4), 393-407. DOI 10.1057/s41262-020-00187-6.** [META+ABS] (Ehrenberg-Bass Institute, University of South Australia.) Design: descriptive survey metric ("Competitive Intensity") on 1,281 in-market brand identity elements from 13 packaged-goods categories in 19 countries. Abstract says "character, logo and logotypes have the greatest potential for unique brand ownership" and that competitive intensity "varies for elements of the same type, suggesting that practitioner execution plays a critical role". Boundary conditions: established CPG brands, uniqueness of brand linkage rather than memory accuracy, descriptive not causal. Grade: moderate (large multi-country descriptive dataset, single paper). — [Springer abstract](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1057/s41262-020-00187-6)

### Inferences
- The stable finding is a dissociation: gist-level recognition/naming is near ceiling, detail-level reproduction is poor. For a design tool this supports "the gist must carry identification; fine detail will not be remembered", and argues against claims that a specific detail makes a logo "memorable".
- The three studies used different logos, countries and scoring, so "strong" applies to the qualitative pattern, not to any percentage. The jump from 1/85 to 18.8% for the same logo is almost certainly a scoring artefact, not a population difference.
- False recognition is treated in Henderson & Cote as a *benefit* for low-budget brands (a logo that feels familiar without exposure). That is the same property that, from a trademark/confusion standpoint, is a risk. The literature fetched here does not quantify confusion with competitors.
- Von Restorff/isolation, picture superiority and dual coding are general memory phenomena; I did not fetch any study that tests them specifically on logos, so applying them to logo rules is extrapolation.

### Gaps
- No fetched logo-specific test of the von Restorff/isolation effect, picture superiority or dual coding. Ward et al.'s reference list points to Childers & Houston (1984, JCR) and Nelson, Reed & Walling (1976) for picture superiority, but I did not verify or read them.
- No peer-reviewed, experimentally manipulated evidence on "simplicity increases recall" was verified; van Grinsven & Das (2016) and Janiszewski & Meyvis (2001) are the candidates and both need full-text reading for direction and effect size.
- Effect sizes for design-property-to-recognition links in Henderson & Cote (1998) were not accessible (abstract only).
- No verified study of false recognition driven by similarity to a competitor's logo.

## 2. Distinctiveness vs typicality: fluency against standing out

### Takeaway
The best field evidence for "typical designs are liked and sell" comes from car fronts, not logos, and the same research group shows the advantage reverses with repeated exposure. There is no verified logo-specific test of the typicality/atypicality trade-off, so any rule here is a transfer from product design.

### Cited Findings
- **Landwehr, J. R., Labroo, A. A., & Herrmann, A. (2011). Gut Liking for the Ordinary: Incorporating Design Fluency Improves Automobile Sales Forecasts. *Marketing Science*, 30(3), 416-429. DOI 10.1287/mksc.1110.0633.** [META+ABS] Design: sales-forecast modelling with objective image measures. Sample: frontal photographs of 28 car models (market: authors in Switzerland/USA; sales market not stated in abstract, UNVERIFIED). Abstract says adding objective prototypicality (distance to the morph) and complexity (compressed file size) "improves their prediction by up to 19%", and "prototypical but complex car designs feel surprisingly fluent to process ... and positively impact car sales"; holds for economy and premium cars. Boundary conditions: cars, 28 units, observational, "up to" figure. Grade: moderate, and **not logo evidence**. — [Crossref record](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1287/mksc.1110.0633)
- **Landwehr, J. R., Wentzel, D., & Herrmann, A. (2013). Product Design for the Long Run: Consumer Responses to Typical and Atypical Designs at Different Stages of Exposure. *Journal of Marketing*, 77(5), 92-107. DOI 10.1509/jm.11.0286.** [META+ABS] Design: three studies in the automobile domain (survey/experiment, real sales data, experiment). Abstract says: "aesthetic liking of typical car designs is greater at lower exposure levels, whereas people like atypical car designs better at higher exposure levels"; Study 2 with real sales data "suggest[s] that atypical cars may be more successful in the long run". Sample sizes not in abstract (UNVERIFIED). Grade: moderate; not logo evidence. — [Crossref record](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1509/jm.11.0286)
- **Ward, Yang, Romaniuk & Beal (2020)** (see section 1): uniqueness of brand linkage differs widely between element types and between executions of the same type. [META+ABS] — [Springer abstract](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1057/s41262-020-00187-6)
- **Dew, R., Ansari, A., & Toubia, O. (2022). Letting Logos Speak: Leveraging Multiview Representation Learning for Data-Driven Branding and Logo Design. *Marketing Science*, 41(2), 401-425. DOI 10.1287/mksc.2021.1326.** [META+ABS; abstract from the SSRN version, DOI 10.2139/ssrn.3406857] The model is explicitly used "for suggesting typical logo features for a brand", i.e. it formalises category typicality of logo features; it does not test whether typical logos perform better. — [Crossref record](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1287/mksc.2021.1326); [SSRN record](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.2139/ssrn.3406857)
- **Montoya, R. M., Horton, R. S., Vevea, J. L., Citkowicz, M., & Lauber, E. A. (2017). A re-examination of the mere exposure effect: The influence of repeated exposure on recognition, familiarity, and liking. *Psychological Bulletin*, 143(5), 459-498. DOI 10.1037/bul0000085.** [META+ABS] Meta-analysis: 268 curve estimates from 81 articles. Abstract says the effect "was characterized by a positive slope and negative quadratic effect consistent with an inverted-U shaped curve", found for "all visual, but not auditory stimuli", and concludes "existing models for the mere exposure effect do not adequately account for the findings". Grade: **strong** for "repeated exposure raises liking of visual stimuli up to a point, then declines". — [PubMed abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=Montoya+Horton+re-examination+mere+exposure+effect+recognition+familiarity+liking&format=abstract)

### Inferences
- Read together, Landwehr 2011 and 2013 say the typicality advantage is an early-exposure effect. Logos are, by design, high-exposure stimuli, so if the car result transfers at all, it weakens "be typical" as a logo rule rather than supporting it.
- Mere exposure being inverted-U and meta-analytically robust means single-exposure lab ratings of unfamiliar logos (the dominant paradigm) systematically measure the wrong point of the curve for a real brand.
- A defensible tool position: report category typicality as information (in the spirit of Dew et al.) without scoring it as good or bad.

### Gaps
- No verified peer-reviewed study manipulating logo typicality/atypicality with recognition or choice outcomes.
- Romaniuk's distinctive-asset framework is mainly in books and Ehrenberg-Bass industry reports; only Ward et al. (2020) was verified as peer-reviewed.
- Landwehr sample sizes and effect sizes were not available from abstracts.

## 3. Cross-cultural generalizability

### Takeaway
The structure of logo perception (elaborateness, naturalness, harmony) and the direction of responses to it look broadly similar across ten countries, with country clusters differing only in degree; that is one large study plus an Asian precursor I could verify only bibliographically. Shape-meaning effects (round vs angular) and proportion preferences rest on single papers, and the one high-powered non-Western replication of a round/angular logo effect found nothing.

### Cited Findings
- **van der Lans et al. (2009), *Marketing Science*, 28(5), 968-985, DOI 10.1287/mksc.1080.0462.** [META+ABS] Design: Bayesian finite-mixture structural equation model on consumer evaluations of logos. Sample: 10 countries (Argentina, Australia, China, Germany, Great Britain, India, The Netherlands, Russia, Singapore, United States); N not in abstract (UNVERIFIED). Abstract says: the model "reduces the 10 countries to three cross-national clusters that respond differently to logo design dimensions: the West, Asia, and Russia"; "elaborateness, naturalness, and harmony are universal design dimensions"; responses (affect, shared meaning, subjective familiarity, true and false recognition) to dimensions and elements (repetition, proportion, parallelism) "are also relatively consistent, although we find minor differences across clusters"; "managers can implement a global logo strategy". Boundary conditions: unfamiliar logos, rating responses, samples per country not described in abstract, data collected before 2009. Grade: **moderate** (large multi-country single paper; no independent replication found). — [Crossref record](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1287/mksc.1080.0462)
- **Henderson, P. W., Cote, J. A., Leong, S. M., & Schmitt, B. (2003). Building strong brands in Asia: selecting the visual components of image to maximize brand strength. *International Journal of Research in Marketing*, 20(4), 297-313. DOI 10.1016/j.ijresmar.2003.03.001.** [META] Abstract could not be fetched from Crossref, OpenAlex or Semantic Scholar (elided); an SSRN copy exists (abstract_id=348520) but was not read. Any statement of its findings (commonly summarised as: design dimensions and their effects in China and Singapore resemble the US pattern) is UNVERIFIED. Grade: unassessed; treat as a precursor of van der Lans et al. — [Crossref record](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1016/j.ijresmar.2003.03.001)
- **Zhang, Y., Feick, L., & Price, L. J. (2006). The Impact of Self-Construal on Aesthetic Preference for Angular Versus Rounded Shapes. *Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin*, 32(6), 794-805. DOI 10.1177/0146167206286626.** [META+ABS] Design: one field study classifying logos from several countries plus two experiments priming self-construal. Abstract says independents "should perceive angular shapes as more attractive", interdependents rounded shapes, the effect "more pronounced when people expect that their shape preferences will be evaluated by others", and that the hypotheses "were largely confirmed". N and countries not in abstract (UNVERIFIED). Boundary conditions: priming paradigm (a class of manipulation with a poor replication record), "largely" confirmed, public-evaluation moderator. Grade: **weak-to-moderate** (multi-study, no replication found, mechanism via priming). — [Crossref record](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1177/0146167206286626)
- **Pittard, N., Ewing, M., & Jevons, C. (2007). Aesthetic theory and logo design: examining consumer response to proportion across cultures. *International Marketing Review*, 24(4), 457-473. DOI 10.1108/02651330710761026.** [META+ABS] Design: survey in Australia, Singapore and South Africa; N not in abstract (UNVERIFIED). Abstract says: "there is universal preference for the divine proportion across cultures. Logos based on forms found in nature that were expressed in the divine proportion were most preferred, but for artificially constructed logos, a 1:1 ratio was preferred." Stated limitation: "A limited set of ratios were considered." Grade: **weak** (single survey, limited ratio set; and its own result contradicts a blanket golden-ratio rule, since abstract/geometric marks preferred 1:1). — [Crossref record](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1108/02651330710761026)
- **Machado, J. C., Vacas de Carvalho, L., Torres, A., & Costa, P. (2015). Brand logo design: examining consumer response to naturalness. *Journal of Product & Brand Management*, 24(1), 78-87. DOI 10.1108/JPBM-05-2014-0609.** [META+ABS] Design: survey, 96 logos classified as abstract, cultural or organic; two studies, 220 participants in Portugal. Abstract says "natural logos are clearly preferred to abstract logos" and "organic designs are favored over cultural designs"; also claims affect toward unknown organic logos equals affect toward well-known abstract logos. Boundary conditions: one country, affect ratings only, convenience sample. Grade: moderate for "natural > abstract on liking" because it agrees with Henderson & Cote (1998) and van der Lans et al. (2009); weak for the organic/cultural split. — [OpenAlex record](https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.1108/jpbm-05-2014-0609)
- **Motoki, K., & Iseki, S. (2022). Evaluating replicability of ten influential research on sensory marketing. *Frontiers in Communication*, 7, 1048896. DOI 10.3389/fcomm.2022.1048896.** [FULLTEXT] Preregistered, N = 823 Japanese online participants (Crowdworks). Replication of Jiang et al. (2015, JCR) Experiment 1 (original n = 109 undergraduates, F(2,106) = 7.63, r = .251): no logo-shape by attribute interaction, F(2,820) = 0.141, p = .868, partial eta squared = .000; circular vs angular logo on comfort M = 5.33 vs 5.34, on durability M = 5.37 vs 5.37. The authors frame the sample as "non-WEIRD", so failure may reflect non-replicability or non-generalizability; the design cannot separate them. — [Frontiers article](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/communication/articles/10.3389/fcomm.2022.1048896/full)

### Inferences
- What appears to travel: the perceptual dimensions themselves and a general liking for natural, harmonious, moderately elaborate marks. What is not established across cultures: semantic inferences from shape (round = soft/warm, angular = hard/competent) and any numerical proportion.
- "Universal" in van der Lans et al. means "same sign, different magnitude in three clusters". A tool that encodes magnitudes or thresholds is going beyond the data.
- Country coverage in the whole verified set: no Africa except South Africa, no Middle East, Latin America only via Argentina. Scripts other than Latin (and their effect on wordmarks) are not addressed in anything I verified.

### Gaps
- Henderson et al. (2003) findings unverified (abstract inaccessible).
- Per-country N and effect sizes for van der Lans et al. not available from the abstract.
- No verified direct replication of Zhang, Feick & Price (2006) or Pittard et al. (2007).
- No verified evidence on right-to-left or logographic scripts.

## 4. Real-world outcomes of logo design

### Takeaway
There is no verified randomised field experiment (A/B test) of a logo change with behavioural or sales outcomes. The "real-world" evidence consists of correlational brand-performance analyses, stock-market event studies of rebranding announcements (which bundle logo with name and strategy), and descriptive machine-learning models; all are compatible with small effects and none isolates logo design causally.

### Cited Findings
- **Park, C. W., Eisingerich, A. B., Pol, G., & Park, J. W. (2013). The role of brand logos in firm performance. *Journal of Business Research*, 66(2), 180-187. DOI 10.1016/j.jbusres.2012.07.011.** [META+ABS] Abstract says positive effects of logos on customer brand commitment and firm performance "derive not from enabling brand identification ... but primarily from facilitating customer self-identity/expressiveness, representing a brand's functional benefits, and offering aesthetic appeal"; also compares brand-name-only logos with visual symbols and tests moderation by brand extension. Design and N are not in the abstract (UNVERIFIED; commonly described as a consumer survey linked to firm financials). Boundary conditions: cross-sectional and correlational; perceived logo benefits are self-reported by customers of existing brands, so reverse causation (liked/successful brands have better-rated logos) is not excluded. Grade: **weak-to-moderate** for any causal reading. — [RePEc abstract](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jbrese/v66y2013i2p180-187.html)
- **Luffarelli, J., Mukesh, M., & Mahmood, A. (2019). Let the Logo Do the Talking: The Influence of Logo Descriptiveness on Brand Equity. *Journal of Marketing Research*, 56(5), 862-878. DOI 10.1177/0022243719845000.** [META+ABS] Design: "multimethod research approach across six studies" plus "an analysis of 597 brand logos". Abstract says more descriptive logos "can positively influence brand evaluations, purchase intentions, and brand performance" via ease of processing and perceived authenticity; "the positive effects ... are considerably attenuated for brands that are familiar (vs. unfamiliar) to consumers and reversed (i.e., negative) for brands that market a type of product/service linked with negatively ... valenced associations". Sample sizes/countries/effect sizes not in abstract (UNVERIFIED). Grade: **moderate** (six studies, one paper, no independent replication found). Key boundary: the effect is mainly for unfamiliar brands. — [Crossref record](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1177/0022243719845000)
- **Luffarelli, J., Stamatogiannakis, A., & Yang, H. (2019; online 2018). The Visual Asymmetry Effect: An Interplay of Logo Design and Brand Personality on Brand Equity. *Journal of Marketing Research*, 56(1), 89-103. DOI 10.1177/0022243718820548.** [META+ABS] Design: "Five studies using a variety of experimental approaches and secondary data sets". Abstract says asymmetrical logos "tend to be more arousing, leading to increased perceptions of excitement", are seen as more congruent with exciting brands, and this "can boost consumers' evaluations and the market's financial valuations of such brands"; the interplay "occurs only for the personality of excitement and the visual property of asymmetry". Grade: **moderate** (multi-study, single lab, an interaction effect; interactions are the least replicable class in the Motoki & Iseki audit). — [Crossref record](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1177/0022243718820548)
- **Dew, Ansari & Toubia (2022), *Marketing Science*, 41(2), 401-425, DOI 10.1287/mksc.2021.1326.** [META+ABS via SSRN version] A multimodal variational autoencoder linking logo image features, text, brand-personality ratings and firm tags; used "for suggesting typical logo features for a brand, and for predicting consumers' reactions to new brands or rebranding efforts". It is descriptive/predictive of associations between logo features and brand perceptions; the abstract reports no sales or performance outcome and makes no causal claim. Grade: moderate as description of what logos brands of a given type tend to have; not evidence that any feature causes an outcome. — [SSRN record](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.2139/ssrn.3406857)
- **Zhao, Y., Calantone, R. J., & Voorhees, C. M. (2018). Identity change vs. strategy change: the effects of rebranding announcements on stock returns. *Journal of the Academy of Marketing Science*, 46(5), 795-812. DOI 10.1007/s11747-018-0579-4.** [META+ABS] Design: event study of 215 rebranding announcements. Abstract says "rebranding decisions, on average, are associated with positive abnormal stock returns in the (−5, +5) event window", moderated by fit among rebranding dimensions, competitive position and industry intensity. Boundary: "brand identity change" bundles name and logo; magnitude not in abstract (UNVERIFIED). Grade: moderate for rebranding announcements, weak for logo design specifically. — [Springer abstract](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11747-018-0579-4)
- **Wang, Liu & Chen (2023). The impacts of logo dynamism and product types on brand market performance. *Journal of Business Research*, 166, 114060. DOI 10.1016/j.jbusres.2023.114060.** [META+ABS; first names not captured] Design: pilot on Forbes most-valuable brands, an event study of abnormal returns after logo-dynamism changes in the Hong Kong stock market, and two experiments. Abstract says "hedonic products with high-dynamic logos and utilitarian products with low-dynamic logos exhibit better market performance". Caveat: it builds on Cian, Krishna & Elder (2014), whose main effect failed a high-powered replication (section 5). Grade: weak. — [ScienceDirect abstract](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0148296323004186)
- **Walsh, M. F., Winterich, K. P., & Mittal, V. (2010). Do logo redesigns help or hurt your brand? The role of brand commitment. *Journal of Product & Brand Management*, 19(2), 76-84. DOI 10.1108/10610421011033421.** [META+ABS] Design: "field experiment with 632 respondents" on New Balance and Adidas (it is an experiment with real brands; outcome is attitude, not behaviour). Abstract says the greater the change toward roundedness of a previously angular logo, the more negatively strongly committed consumers evaluate it, while "weakly committed consumers react positively". Grade: moderate (one large study, two brands, one category). — [Crossref record](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1108/10610421011033421)
- **Bossel, V., Geyskens, K., & Goukens, C. (2019). Facing a trend of brand logo simplicity: The impact of brand logo design on consumption. *Food Quality and Preference*, 71, 129-135. DOI 10.1016/j.foodqual.2018.06.009.** [META+ABS] Design: two experiments with actual food intake as outcome. Abstract says people "consume more from a product which features a simple, flat logo compared to a product that features a complex, non-flat logo", but "for non-prestigious products simple brand logos increase consumption, the opposite occurs for prestigious food products". N/country not in abstract (UNVERIFIED). Grade: weak (two small-scale experiments, a reversal by product prestige, no replication). This is the only verified peer-reviewed behavioural evidence touching the flat/minimalist redesign trend, and it is not about brand equity. — [Semantic Scholar record](https://api.semanticscholar.org/graph/v1/paper/DOI:10.1016/j.foodqual.2018.06.009?fields=title,abstract,year,venue)
- **Müller, B., Kocher, B., & Crettaz, A. (2013). The effects of visual rejuvenation through brand logos. *Journal of Business Research*, 66(1), 82-88. DOI 10.1016/j.jbusres.2011.07.026.** [META] Findings UNVERIFIED (abstract elided). — [Crossref query result](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1016/j.jbusres.2011.07.026)

### Inferences
- The causal chain a practitioner cares about (design feature -> behaviour -> revenue) is not closed by any single verified study. Experiments establish feature -> rating for unfamiliar brands; secondary data establish feature <-> performance correlation for existing brands.
- Three verified moderators all point the same way: effects shrink or reverse for familiar brands (Luffarelli, Mukesh & Mahmood), for committed customers (Walsh et al.), and for prestige products (Bossel et al.). Logo-design rules therefore apply most to new/unknown brands and least to established ones.
- Stock-market event studies measure investor reaction to an announcement, not consumer reaction to a design.

### Gaps
- No verified peer-reviewed A/B test or randomised field experiment of a logo change with sales/conversion outcomes.
- No verified peer-reviewed study of "blanding" (convergent sans-serif minimalist redesigns) with brand-equity outcomes; I found only trade commentary and the Bossel et al. consumption study.
- Effect sizes for Park et al. (2013) and both Luffarelli papers were not accessible from abstracts.
- A 2021 *Journal of Brand Management* event study of name and logo changes at Indian banks (DOI 10.1057/s41262-021-00266-2) appeared in search results but was not verified or read.

## 5. Replicability and effect sizes of the logo-psychology literature

### Takeaway
I found no meta-analysis, registered report or many-labs study of logo-design effects. The only two logo-specific findings subjected to a high-powered preregistered replication (circular vs angular logo shape; logo dynamism) both failed with effects indistinguishable from zero, and the base rate for marketing as tracked by OpenMKT is 5 successes in 45 direct replications. The supporting general mechanism with real meta-analytic backing is mere exposure, and it is an inverted U, not "more is better".

### Cited Findings
- **OpenMKT replication tracker (Charlton, maintained web resource, not peer-reviewed).** [FULLTEXT of web page] "So far, 5 out of 45 high-powered, direct replications of marketing studies have been successful." Success criterion: significant in the same direction as the original and free of obvious confounds. Original journals are mostly JCR, JMR, JCP. Selection is not random (many entries come from Data Colada's targeted "Data Replicada" series and the O'Donnell et al. 2021 audit), so 11% is not an unbiased estimate of the field's replication rate. — [OpenMKT tracker](https://openmkt.org/research/replications-of-marketing-studies/)
- **Jiang, Y., Gorn, G. J., Galli, M., & Chattopadhyay, A. (2016; online 2015). Does Your Company Have the Right Logo? How and Why Circular- and Angular-Logo Shapes Influence Brand Attribute Judgments. *Journal of Consumer Research*, 42(5), 709-726. DOI 10.1093/jcr/ucv049.** [META+ABS] Five experiments; abstract claims circular vs angular logos "activate softness and hardness associations" that shape product/company attribute judgments, with the effect disappearing under visual load, low imagery disposition, or a conflicting headline. **Replication: failed.** Motoki & Iseki (2022) replicated Experiment 1 (original n = 109 undergraduates, r = .251) with N = 823: F(2,820) = 0.141, p = .868, partial eta squared = .000. Listed by OpenMKT as "Unambiguous success: No". Grade: **weak / contested**. — [OpenAlex abstract](https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.1093/jcr/ucv049); [OpenMKT entry 51](https://openmkt.org/research/replications-of-marketing-studies/entry/51); [Frontiers article](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/communication/articles/10.3389/fcomm.2022.1048896/full)
- **Cian, L., Krishna, A., & Elder, R. S. (2014). This Logo Moves Me: Dynamic Imagery from Static Images. *Journal of Marketing Research*, 51(2), 184-197. DOI 10.1509/jmr.13.0023.** [META+ABS] Abstract claims perceived movement in a static logo raises engagement and brand attitudes, moderated by congruence with brand characteristics. **Replication: failed on the outcome.** Motoki & Iseki (2022), Study 1 (original n = 74 US undergraduates, F(1,72) = 5.26, r = .261): brand attitude high vs low dynamism M = 5.64 vs 5.59, F(1,821) = 0.393, p = .531, partial eta squared = .001; the manipulation check did work (perceived movement 5.85 vs 5.00, F(1,821) = 77.863), so people saw the movement and their attitudes did not change. Grade: **weak / contested**. — [Crossref record](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1509/jmr.13.0023); [OpenMKT entry 77](https://openmkt.org/research/replications-of-marketing-studies/entry/77); [Frontiers article](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/communication/articles/10.3389/fcomm.2022.1048896/full)
- **Motoki & Iseki (2022), overall.** [FULLTEXT] Abstract: "only 20% of findings could be successfully replicated, and their effect sizes are as half as the original ones... No studies involving visual factors, between-participant manipulation of senses, or interactions between factors could be replicated." Body: replication effect sizes were smaller than the originals in all ten studies; for most of the eight failures the original study's estimated power had a 95% CI including values below 20%. Limits: one replication team, Japanese online sample with translated materials, one study per paper, Frontiers journal. — [Frontiers article](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/communication/articles/10.3389/fcomm.2022.1048896/full)
- **Urminsky, O., & Dietvorst, B. J. (2024). Taking the Full Measure: Integrating Replication into Research Practice to Assess Generalizability. *Journal of Consumer Research*, 51(1), 157-168. DOI 10.1093/jcr/ucae007.** [META+ABS] Review of replication practice in JCR; the 2022 working-paper version states that self-replication within papers has grown but "Independent replication, however, has remained rare." — [Crossref record](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1093/jcr/ucae007); [working paper PDF](https://home.uchicago.edu/ourminsky/Once%20More%20Unto%20the%20Breach.pdf)
- **Open Science Collaboration (2015). Estimating the reproducibility of psychological science. *Science*, 349(6251). DOI 10.1126/science.aac4716.** [META+ABS (editor's summary)] 100 experiments from three psychology journals; "about one-third to one-half of the original findings were also observed in the replication study." — [Crossref record](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1126/science.aac4716)
- **Camerer et al. (2018), "Evaluating the replicability of social science experiments in Nature and Science between 2010 and 2015".** [META+ABS for the SocArXiv preprint, DOI 10.31235/osf.io/4hmb6; the journal version in *Nature Human Behaviour* was NOT fetched, so its DOI/pages are UNVERIFIED] 21 studies, preregistered, about five times the original sample: significant same-direction effect in 13 (62%), "the effect size of the replications is on average about 50% of the original effect size". — [Crossref preprint record](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.31235/osf.io/4hmb6)
- **Eisend, M. (2015). Have We Progressed Marketing Knowledge? A Meta-Meta-Analysis of Effect Sizes in Marketing Research. *Journal of Marketing*, 79(3), 23-40. DOI 10.1509/jm.14.0288.** [META+ABS] 176 meta-analyses, more than 7,500 primary studies (1918-2012), 1,841 meta-analytic effect sizes; "meta-meta-analytic correlation of .24"; effect sizes vary by area. This is an average of *published* effects and is not corrected for publication bias in the abstract. — [Crossref record](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1509/jm.14.0288)
- **Montoya et al. (2017), *Psychological Bulletin*** (see section 2): mere exposure is meta-analytically supported for visual stimuli with an inverted-U shape; existing theoretical models "do not adequately account for the findings". [META+ABS] — [PubMed abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=Montoya+Horton+re-examination+mere+exposure+effect+recognition+familiarity+liking&format=abstract)
- **Bornstein, R. F. (1989). Exposure and affect: Overview and meta-analysis of research, 1968-1987. *Psychological Bulletin*, 106(2), 265-289. DOI 10.1037/0033-2909.106.2.265.** [META] Only a French-language summary was retrievable (it says the author analyses effects of stimulus type and complexity, presentation interval, exposure time, and subject variables). The commonly quoted overall effect size (r of about .26) is UNVERIFIED here. — [OpenAlex record](https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.1037/0033-2909.106.2.265)
- **Henrich, J., Heine, S. J., & Norenzayan, A. (2010). The weirdest people in the world? *Behavioral and Brain Sciences*, 33(2-3), 61-83. DOI 10.1017/S0140525X0999152X.** [META] Cited only as the standard reference for WEIRD-sample dependence; content not fetched. — [Crossref record](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1017/s0140525x0999152x)
- Sample profile of the logo experiments actually verified here: Jiang et al. Experiment 1 n = 109 undergraduates; Cian et al. Study 1 n = 74 US undergraduates; Blake et al. N = 85 UCLA students; Iancu & Iancu N = 198 Romanian students. — [Frontiers article, Table 1](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/communication/articles/10.3389/fcomm.2022.1048896/full); [Crossref record for Blake et al.](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1080/17470218.2014.1002798)

### Inferences
- Practitioner effect-size expectation. Original logo experiments report effects around r = .25 (d about 0.5) from cells of roughly 25-55 participants. Large replications in neighbouring literatures return about half the original effect on average (Camerer et al.; Motoki & Iseki for the ones that replicate at all), and the two logo-specific replications returned r of about 0. A defensible planning value for a single logo design feature on attitude toward an unfamiliar brand is therefore somewhere between zero and r = .10 (d of 0.2 or less), and smaller again for familiar brands given the moderators in section 4. This range is my inference from those sources, not a published estimate.
- Interaction claims ("feature X works for brand type Y") are the weakest class: none replicated in Motoki & Iseki, and they need far larger samples than main effects. Most "congruence" findings in logo research (asymmetry x excitement, dynamism x product type, descriptiveness x familiarity) are interactions.
- Recurrent threats to validity in the verified studies: student or online-panel samples from a few countries; fictitious or unfamiliar brands; a single exposure lasting seconds; self-reported attitudes or intentions rather than behaviour; within-paper "replications" by the same lab; judge-rated design variables.
- The failed Jiang et al. replication does not prove that round/angular shapes carry no meaning anywhere; it shows that the specific downstream claim (shape changes product-attribute judgments) did not appear in a large Japanese sample. Crossmodal shape-softness correspondences as a perceptual phenomenon were not assessed in this note.
- OpenMKT's 5/45 should be quoted with its selection caveat; it is nevertheless well below the 36-62% found in broader psychology/social-science replication projects, which is a reason to discount unreplicated JCR/JMR/JCP logo findings more heavily than the general literature.

### Gaps
- No meta-analysis specific to logo design effects was found (searches via Crossref and web search, 2020-2026).
- No registered report or multi-lab replication of any logo effect was found.
- No fetched meta-analysis of processing-fluency effects on liking. Reber, Schwarz & Winkielman (2004, *Personality and Social Psychology Review*, 8(4), 364-382, DOI 10.1207/s15327957pspr0804_3) was verified bibliographically only [META]; it is a theory review, not a meta-analysis.
- Data Colada posts were not fetched directly; their replications are represented only through the OpenMKT table.
- Bornstein (1989) effect size unverified.
- I could not verify a published, bias-corrected typical effect size for consumer-psychology experiments.

## 6. Existing reviews of the logo literature

### Takeaway
The one substantial review is a narrative classification of 124 studies; it describes the field as fragmented and does not pool effect sizes or assess replicability. Later "systematic reviews" found by search are in low-tier or preprint outlets and were not verified.

### Cited Findings
- **Kim, M. J., & Lim, J. H. (2019). A comprehensive review on logo literature: research topics, findings, and future directions. *Journal of Marketing Management*, 35(13-14), 1291-1365. DOI 10.1080/0267257X.2019.1604563.** [META+ABS] Abstract says: "the logo literature remains fragmented"; the authors "review 124 studies published in business journals over the past 30 years, and classify them into six major research topics: 1) theoretical foundations, 2) logo design/redesign, 3) basic logo elements, 4) additional logo elements, 5) outcomes of logo use, and 6) practical applications of logo use". It is a narrative review restricted to business journals; the abstract mentions no quantitative synthesis, no risk-of-bias or replication assessment. Specific future-research recommendations are in the full text, which was not accessible (UNVERIFIED). — [OpenAlex record](https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.1080/0267257x.2019.1604563)
- Search results (NOT verified, pointers only): Liang et al. (2024), "Logo Impact on Consumer's Perception, Attitude, Brand Image and Purchase Intention: A 5 Years Systematic Review" (ResearchGate listing); "Effects of Brand Visual Identity on Consumer Attitude: A Systematic Literature Review" (Preprints.org, 2024, reported as covering 34 studies); "Effects of Visual Brand Identity Elements on Consumer Purchase Behavior: A Systematic Literature Review" (ejmeb.com, covering 2004-2024). None was opened; journals and DOIs unconfirmed; do not cite as evidence. — [ResearchGate listing](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/379216337_Logo_Impact_on_Consumer's_Perception_Attitude_Brand_Image_and_Purchase_Intention_A_5_Years_Systematic_Review); [Preprints.org](https://www.preprints.org/manuscript/202405.1109); [ejmeb.com](https://ejmeb.com/index.php/journal/article/view/200)

### Inferences
- Thirty years of logo research produced about 124 business-journal studies spread over six topics; with that many distinct effects and so few studies per effect, quantitative pooling is mostly impossible, which explains the absence of a meta-analysis.
- Reviews in this area summarise what was claimed, not what held up; a tool should not treat "appears in a review" as an evidence grade.

### Gaps
- Kim & Lim's full list of gaps and practitioner guidelines could not be read (publisher paywall; abstract only).
- Foroudi, Melewar & Gupta's corporate-logo work was not searched or verified in this session.
- No bibliometric analysis of logo research was located through Crossref (the query returned unrelated items).

## 7. What a design tool can honestly encode

### Takeaway
Very little of the logo-psychology literature supports a hard rule. A handful of claims deserve "safe" status because they are replicated or meta-analytic and are about memory and exposure rather than about the meaning of shapes; most design-feature claims should be soft, labelled heuristics; shape-meaning and proportion rules should not be encoded.

### Cited Findings
Each item points to the evidence above; grades are mine, derived from the cited sources.

**Safe to encode as a rule (strong: replicated or meta-analytic)**
- People do not retain logo detail and overestimate how well they do; identification must work from the overall gist/silhouette, not from fine features. Blake et al. 2015 (N = 85, USA), Iancu & Iancu 2017 (N = 198, Romania), Mayas et al. 2023 (Spain). — [Blake](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1080/17470218.2014.1002798); [Iancu](https://doi.org/10.17646/kome.2017.24); [Mayas](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1002/acp.4138)
- Liking of a visual stimulus rises with repeated exposure and then falls (inverted U); first-impression ratings of an unfamiliar logo are therefore a poor proxy for its long-run evaluation. Montoya et al. 2017 (268 curves, 81 articles). — [PubMed abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=Montoya+Horton+re-examination+mere+exposure+effect+recognition+familiarity+liking&format=abstract)
- Meta-rule for the tool itself: any single-study logo effect should be displayed with a "small and uncertain" label; replication effect sizes run at about half the originals and many go to zero. Camerer et al. 2018 preprint; Motoki & Iseki 2022; OpenMKT. — [Camerer preprint](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.31235/osf.io/4hmb6); [Motoki & Iseki](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/communication/articles/10.3389/fcomm.2022.1048896/full); [OpenMKT](https://openmkt.org/research/replications-of-marketing-studies/)

**Encode only as a soft heuristic (moderate: multi-study single paper, or large single dataset; state the boundary condition)**
- Natural (representational), harmonious, moderately elaborate marks tend to be liked and correctly recognised; the direction is similar across 10 countries, magnitudes differ. Henderson & Cote 1998 (195 logos); van der Lans et al. 2009; Machado et al. 2015 (220 participants, Portugal). Boundary: unfamiliar logos, ratings, correlational. — [Henderson & Cote](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1177/002224299806200202); [van der Lans](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1287/mksc.1080.0462); [Machado](https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.1108/jpbm-05-2014-0609)
- Highly harmonious but less natural marks produce a false sense of familiarity: useful for a low-budget brand, and a warning sign for confusability. Henderson & Cote 1998. — [Henderson & Cote](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1177/002224299806200202)
- A logo that describes the product/service helps evaluations of an *unfamiliar* brand; the benefit fades for familiar brands and reverses for categories with negative associations. Luffarelli, Mukesh & Mahmood 2019 (six studies + 597 logos). — [Crossref record](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1177/0022243719845000)
- Redesigns: the larger the change, the more negatively strongly committed customers react, while weakly committed ones react positively. Walsh, Winterich & Mittal 2010 (632 respondents, two shoe brands). — [Crossref record](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1108/10610421011033421)
- Logos, logotypes and characters are the identity elements most capable of being uniquely linked to one brand, and execution matters more than element type. Ward et al. 2020 (1,281 elements, 13 categories, 19 countries). Boundary: established packaged-goods brands, descriptive. — [Springer abstract](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1057/s41262-020-00187-6)
- Category typicality: show it as information, not as a score. Typical designs are liked early and atypical ones later, in car-design data. Landwehr et al. 2011 (28 car models); Landwehr et al. 2013 (three studies); Dew et al. 2022 for measuring typical logo features. Boundary: the liking evidence is not from logos. — [Landwehr 2011](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1287/mksc.1110.0633); [Landwehr 2013](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1509/jm.11.0286); [Dew et al.](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1287/mksc.2021.1326)
- Asymmetry fits brands positioned as exciting. Luffarelli, Stamatogiannakis & Yang 2019 (five studies). Label as an unreplicated interaction, weakest item in this tier. — [Crossref record](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1177/0022243718820548)

**Do not encode (weak: failed replication, single contested study, or contradicted by its own data)**
- "Round logos signal softness/comfort, angular logos signal hardness/durability" as a driver of product or company judgments. Jiang et al. 2016 failed a preregistered N = 823 replication with partial eta squared = .000. — [OpenMKT entry 51](https://openmkt.org/research/replications-of-marketing-studies/entry/51); [Frontiers article](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/communication/articles/10.3389/fcomm.2022.1048896/full)
- "Dynamic-looking logos improve brand attitude", and its derivative "dynamic for hedonic, static for utilitarian". Cian et al. 2014 failed on the attitude outcome (partial eta squared = .001) even though the movement manipulation worked; Wang et al. 2023 builds on it. — [OpenMKT entry 77](https://openmkt.org/research/replications-of-marketing-studies/entry/77); [ScienceDirect abstract](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0148296323004186)
- "Use the golden ratio." The only verified cross-cultural test (Pittard et al. 2007, three countries, limited ratio set) found the 1.618 preference only for nature-based forms and a preference for 1:1 for constructed marks. — [Crossref record](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1108/02651330710761026)
- "Collectivist cultures prefer round, individualist cultures prefer angular" as a localisation rule. Zhang, Feick & Price 2006: one field classification plus two priming experiments, "largely confirmed", no replication found. — [Crossref record](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1177/0146167206286626)
- "Simpler/flatter logos perform better." The only verified behavioural study (Bossel et al. 2019) found the effect reverses for prestige products; the Apple-logo studies show extreme simplicity does not guarantee accurate recall. — [Semantic Scholar record](https://api.semanticscholar.org/graph/v1/paper/DOI:10.1016/j.foodqual.2018.06.009?fields=title,abstract,year,venue); [Blake et al.](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1080/17470218.2014.1002798)
- "A good logo raises firm performance / share price." Park et al. 2013 is cross-sectional; event studies cover rebranding announcements, not design. — [RePEc abstract](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jbrese/v66y2013i2p180-187.html); [Springer abstract](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11747-018-0579-4)

### Inferences
- The safe tier contains statements about human memory and exposure, plus a rule about the tool's own humility. Nothing about what a particular shape "means" reaches it.
- Every soft heuristic should carry its boundary condition in the UI text, the most common one being "evidence is from unfamiliar brands rated once".
- An honest grade label needs three fields: evidence tier, population/stimulus the evidence comes from, and whether an independent replication exists (for almost every item: no).

### Gaps
- Typography, colour (excluded by scope) and name-sound effects are not covered by this note.
- Several papers were verified bibliographically only (Henderson et al. 2003; van Grinsven & Das 2016; Müller et al. 2013; Bornstein 1989; Reber et al. 2004; Henrich et al. 2010) and should be read in full before any rule cites them.
- Effect sizes (d, r, partial eta squared) are available here only for the two replicated-and-failed studies; for every "moderate" item the magnitude is unknown from abstracts and must not be shown as a number in the tool.

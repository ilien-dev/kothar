# Meaning-related and compositional properties of logos: effects on consumer responses

Verification protocol used for these notes (read before using any number):

- Every citation below was checked against a fetched bibliographic record (OpenAlex API, which mirrors Crossref DOI metadata) for title, authors, year, journal, volume/pages and DOI. Abstract text was fetched from OpenAlex, Semantic Scholar, Springer Nature Link or IDEAS/RePEc. All web access went through Svipall.
- Full text was obtained for ONE paper only (Luffarelli, Mukesh & Mahmood 2019, accepted manuscript from the University of Sussex repository). For every other paper, the statements below are what the ABSTRACT says. Where the abstract gives no N, country or statistic, this is written as "not in abstract" rather than filled from memory.
- "Country" is the sample country only when the abstract states it. Author-affiliation countries are not reported as sample countries.
- Effect sizes marked "computed" are my own conversions from reported F tests (partial eta squared = F / (F + df_error); d ≈ 2·sqrt(F/df_error)); they are inferences, not author-reported values.
- Evidence grades follow the brief: strong = replicated/meta-analytic; moderate = multi-study single paper; weak = single study or contested. No meta-analysis of any property in this file was found (see last section), so "strong" is never awarded on meta-analytic grounds; where several independent papers converge I say "moderate, conceptually replicated".
- Publisher full texts on SAGE, OUP, Elsevier, Emerald and Taylor & Francis were paywalled or blocked (ScienceDirect returned a human-verification challenge), so per-study Ns and test statistics for those papers are a known gap.

## 1. Descriptive vs non-descriptive logos (Luffarelli, Mukesh & Mahmood 2019) and representativeness/meaningfulness

### Takeaway
More descriptive logos (text and/or image that indicates what the brand sells) raise perceived authenticity, brand evaluation and purchase intention for unfamiliar brands, and correlate with higher sales in a 423-brand Compustat sample; the effect shrinks for familiar brands and reverses for negatively valenced categories. The lab effects are medium-sized, but the field/survey and moderation results are statistically fragile (several rely on 90% or 93% confidence intervals).

### Cited Findings

- **F1.1 Descriptiveness raises authenticity, evaluation and purchase intention.** Luffarelli, J., Mukesh, M., & Mahmood, A. (2019). Let the Logo Do the Talking: The Influence of Logo Descriptiveness on Brand Equity. *Journal of Marketing Research, 56*(5), 862–878. DOI 10.1177/0022243719845000. Design: six studies (experiments, a logo survey, secondary financial data). Samples (from the accepted manuscript): Study 1 N = 180 MTurk (2 × 2 between-participants, fictitious basketball-equipment and running-shoe brands); Study 3 used 174 real logos of UK crowdfunding start-ups rated by 1,327 MTurk participants (DVs) and a separate 1,303 MTurk participants (descriptiveness); Study 6 used 423 brands with Compustat 2013 data. Statistics: Study 1 authenticity M = 5.89 vs 4.82 on a 9-point scale, F(1,176) = 17.69, p < .001 (computed partial η² ≈ .09, d ≈ 0.63); Study 2 brand evaluation M = 5.04 vs 4.25, F(1,243) = 12.41, p = .001 (computed partial η² ≈ .05, d ≈ 0.45). Mechanism per abstract: descriptive logos "are easier to process and thus elicit stronger impressions of authenticity". Grade: moderate (multi-study, single paper; no independent replication found). — [abstract/record](https://doi.org/10.1177/0022243719845000); [accepted manuscript](https://sussex.figshare.com/articles/journal_contribution/Let_the_logo_do_the_talking_the_influence_of_logo_descriptiveness_on_brand_equity/23483705)
  - Rule: for a new or unfamiliar brand in a positively valenced category, include a visual or verbal cue to the product/service in the logo.
  - Check: brief-to-concept — ask N naive raters "how descriptive of the product is this logo?" (the paper used a 1–9 scale, 1 = not at all, 9 = very) with the product named; SVG — cannot be computed from geometry alone; proxy = presence of a `<text>`/outlined wordmark containing a category noun, or an image-classifier/VLM label of the symbol matching the category in the brief.

- **F1.2 With real logos and 13 design controls, the effect is small and marginal.** Same paper, Study 3: descriptiveness → authenticity β = .08, t(147) = 1.86, p = .065; authenticity → purchase intention β = .88, t(146) = 14.60, p < .001; indirect effect significant only at the 93% level (93% CI [.001, .14]); residual direct effect n.s. (β = −.04). Grade: weak for this specific estimate (authors themselves flag the 93% level). — [accepted manuscript](https://sussex.figshare.com/articles/journal_contribution/Let_the_logo_do_the_talking_the_influence_of_logo_descriptiveness_on_brand_equity/23483705)
  - Rule: treat descriptiveness as a modest lever, not a dominant one, once other design properties vary.
  - Check: when scoring concepts, weight descriptiveness below fluency/authenticity ratings measured directly.

- **F1.3 Moderator: brand familiarity attenuates the effect.** Same paper, Study 4 (N ≈ 106 from df; 2 × 2): unfamiliar brand M = 6.26 vs 4.41, F(1,102) = 13.75, p < .001; familiar brand M = 5.02 vs 4.28, F(1,102) = 2.82, p = .096; interaction only marginal, F(1,102) = 2.83, p = .096; moderated-mediation index 90% CI [−1.36, −.001]. Grade: weak-to-moderate (interaction not significant at .05). — [accepted manuscript](https://sussex.figshare.com/articles/journal_contribution/Let_the_logo_do_the_talking_the_influence_of_logo_descriptiveness_on_brand_equity/23483705)
  - Rule: descriptiveness matters most for start-ups and unknown brands; established brands gain little.
  - Check: brief field "brand familiarity in target market (low/high)" gates the weight of the descriptiveness criterion.

- **F1.4 Moderator: negative product categories reverse the effect.** Same paper, Study 5 (olive oil vs palm oil; N ≈ 240 from df): interaction F(1,236) = 9.54, p = .002 (computed partial η² ≈ .04); positive category M = 5.50 vs 4.78, p = .022; negative category M = 3.85 vs 4.49, p = .040; mediated moderation via authenticity 95% CI [−1.71, −.35]. Grade: moderate (single study, clear crossover). — [accepted manuscript](https://sussex.figshare.com/articles/journal_contribution/Let_the_logo_do_the_talking_the_influence_of_logo_descriptiveness_on_brand_equity/23483705)
  - Rule: do not depict the product when the category carries negative associations (the paper's example: palm oil); use a non-descriptive mark.
  - Check: brief field "category valence" (pre-test with target consumers); if negative, flag descriptive concepts.

- **F1.5 Sales association and practice gap.** Same paper, Study 6: logo descriptiveness (binary, three coders, 85% agreement) → net sales β = .13, t(387) = 2.12, p = .035; marginal after winsorizing (β = .11, p = .052); gross profit β = .16, p = .005; EBITDA p = .071; net income p = .053. Cross-sectional, authors warn "caution is thus warranted". Of 597 logos across Studies 3 and 6, 41% were descriptive and 59% were not, χ²(1, N = 597) = 18.47, p < .001. Logo type (wordmark vs icon-only vs mixed) did not moderate the descriptiveness effect in Studies 3 and 6; no interaction with brand personality was found. Grade: weak (correlational, cross-sectional). — [accepted manuscript](https://sussex.figshare.com/articles/journal_contribution/Let_the_logo_do_the_talking_the_influence_of_logo_descriptiveness_on_brand_equity/23483705)
  - Rule: descriptiveness can be delivered by either the wordmark or the symbol; the paper found no evidence that mark type changes its value.
  - Check: binary coding (descriptive yes/no) by 3 independent raters, target agreement ≥ 85% as in the paper.

- **F1.6 Naturalness/representativeness in the Henderson–Cote framework.** Henderson, P. W., & Cote, J. A. (1998). Guidelines for Selecting or Modifying Logos. *Journal of Marketing, 62*(2), starting p. 14. DOI 10.1177/002224299806200202. Design: empirical analysis of 195 logos calibrated on 13 design characteristics (rater sample and country not in abstract). Finding per abstract: high-recognition logos "should be very natural, very harmonious, and moderately elaborate"; low-investment logos (false sense of knowing, positive affect) "should be less natural and very harmonious"; high-image logos "must be moderately elaborate and natural". No effect sizes in abstract. Grade: moderate (large stimulus set, single paper; correlational across logos). — [record/abstract](https://doi.org/10.1177/002224299806200202)
  - Rule: prefer natural (representative/organic) forms with harmony and moderate elaborateness when recognition and image are the goals.
  - Check: SVG — elaborateness proxies (path count, node count, number of distinct fills, perimeter²/area complexity); harmony proxies (bilateral symmetry score by mirroring the rasterized mark, bounding-box balance); naturalness still needs human or VLM rating ("depicts a commonly experienced object: yes/no").

- **F1.7 Natural (especially organic) logos are liked more than abstract ones.** Machado, J. C., de Carvalho, L. V., Torres, A., & Costa, P. (2015). Brand logo design: examining consumer response to naturalness. *Journal of Product & Brand Management, 24*(1), 78–87. DOI 10.1108/JPBM-05-2014-0609. Design: 96 logos pre-classified as abstract, cultural or organic; survey, two studies, 220 participants, Portugal. Finding per abstract: "natural logos are clearly preferred to abstract logos", organic favoured over cultural; "affect toward unknown organic logos is at the same level as affect toward well-known abstract logos". No statistics in abstract. Grade: moderate (two studies, one country). — [record/abstract](https://doi.org/10.1108/jpbm-05-2014-0609)
  - Rule: for unknown brands without media budget, organic figurative marks buy affect that abstract marks only get through exposure.
  - Check: classify each concept as abstract / cultural / organic (3-level coding by raters).

- **F1.8 Figurativeness is not universally beneficial; name × logo interaction matters.** Lencastre, P., Machado, J. C., & Costa, P. (2023). The effect of brand names and logos' figurativeness on memory: An experimental approach. *Journal of Business Research, 164*, 113944. DOI 10.1016/j.jbusres.2023.113944. Design: three experimental scenarios with fictitious names and logos from very abstract to very figurative (N and country not in abstract). Finding per abstract: "semantic repetition is beneficial for creating recall and generating associations, and semantic dispersion is advantageous for ensuring recognition"; "figurativeness is not a sacrosanct solution". Grade: weak-to-moderate. — [record/abstract](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jbusres.2023.113944)
  - Rule: choose name–symbol semantic repetition when the goal is recall/associations, and semantic divergence when the goal is recognition.
  - Check: brief states the memory goal (recall vs recognition); code name and symbol each as abstract/figurative and record whether they denote the same concept.

- **F1.9 Visual vs conceptual complexity change sign with exposure.** Miceli, G., Scopelliti, I., Raimondo, M. A., & Donato, C. (2014). Breaking Through Complexity: Visual and Conceptual Dimensions in Logo Evaluation across Exposures. *Psychology & Marketing, 31*(10), 886–899. DOI 10.1002/mar.20741. Design: "hybrid experimental study" (N not in abstract). Finding per abstract: "the initially positive effect of VC [visual complexity] on attitude toward the logo becomes negative with multiple exposures, whereas the initially negative effect of CC [conceptual complexity] on attitude toward the logo becomes positive as exposures increase". Grade: weak (single study). — [record/abstract](https://doi.org/10.1002/mar.20741)
  - Rule: for a logo that will be seen many times, keep visual complexity low and allow multiple meanings (conceptual richness).
  - Check: SVG visual complexity = node count, path count, compressed file size (gzip bytes of the normalised SVG or PNG); conceptual complexity = number of distinct meanings raters list.

- **F1.10 Logo concreteness and stability-related meanings (pointers, abstracts not retrieved).** Zheng, L., & Zhu, Y. (2025). Visualizing brand longevity: How logo concreteness shapes consumer perceptions of brand age. *Journal of Retailing and Consumer Services, 88*, 104495. DOI 10.1016/j.jretconser.2025.104495. Only title/authors/journal verified; findings UNVERIFIED (abstract not retrievable). — [record](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jretconser.2025.104495)

### Inferences
- The descriptiveness effect appears to operate through processing fluency → authenticity; this predicts it should be weakest wherever the brand name or context already tells people what is sold (consistent with the familiarity moderator, but this broader prediction is my inference).
- F1.1 and F1.7 point the same way (meaningful/representative marks help unknown brands), from different labs and methods; F1.8 and F1.9 qualify this by goal and exposure level.
- Because descriptiveness in Study 3 was continuous and in Study 6 binary, a workflow can reasonably use a 1–9 human rating at concept stage and a binary flag for audits.

### Gaps
- Studies 2 and 5 sample sizes are inferred from error degrees of freedom, not read directly; exact per-study N for all six studies should be re-read from the published version.
- No independent or preregistered replication of the descriptiveness effect was found in my searches (search-engine coverage was poor; OpenAlex title searches were the main route).
- I could not retrieve the abstract or full text of Zheng & Zhu (2025).

## 2. Implied motion / dynamism (Cian, Krishna & Elder 2014) and follow-ups

### Takeaway
Static logos that evoke movement increase engagement and thereby brand attitude, but only when dynamism fits the brand; follow-ups add force cues (drag), agent-like animation, diagonal orientation and facing direction, each with fit moderators (dynamic vs stable firm, promotion vs prevention focus, modern vs traditional positioning). Evidence is multi-paper and convergent but every paper is a conceptual, not direct, replication.

### Cited Findings

- **F2.1 Dynamic imagery → engagement → attitude.** Cian, L., Krishna, A., & Elder, R. S. (2014). This Logo Moves Me: Dynamic Imagery from Static Images. *Journal of Marketing Research, 51*(2), 184–197. DOI 10.1509/jmr.13.0023. Design: multiple studies with self-report engagement and eye-tracking (Ns and country not in abstract). Finding per abstract: "the evoked dynamic imagery affects the level of consumer engagement with the brand logo"; engagement "affects consumer attitudes toward the brand"; the chain "is moderated by the congruence between perceived movement and brand characteristics". No effect sizes in abstract. Grade: moderate, conceptually replicated (F2.2–F2.6). — [record/abstract](https://doi.org/10.1509/jmr.13.0023)
  - Rule: add implied motion (a frozen-in-action pose, off-balance or leaning elements) when the brand wants to be seen as modern/active; avoid it for brands positioned on tradition or stability.
  - Check: human rating of perceived movement (the paper measures perceived movement of the logo); SVG proxies — dominant axis angle of the mark (PCA of filled pixels) away from 0°/90°, centroid offset from the base of support, presence of trailing/speed-line elements, italic slant angle of the wordmark.

- **F2.2 Force cues: drag enhances "brand work" and "brand energy".** Baxter, S. M., & Ilicic, J. (2018). May the force drag your dynamic logo: The brand work-energy effect. *International Journal of Research in Marketing, 35*(3), 509–523. DOI 10.1016/j.ijresmar.2018.03.002. Design: four experiments comparing static, kinematic (motion without force) and dynamic (motion with gravitational, spring, air-resistance or tension force) logos (Ns not in abstract). Finding per abstract: "the presence of a drag force in brand logos increases brand attitude and behavior"; mediated by brand work and brand energy "rather than brand engagement"; "a thrust force of air propulsion results in attenuation", high drag magnitude enhances the effect. Grade: moderate; note it disputes Cian et al.'s engagement mechanism. — [record/abstract](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijresmar.2018.03.002)
  - Rule: if you depict motion, show effort against resistance (stretch, tension, drag) rather than effortless propulsion.
  - Check: concept review item "does the mark show a force opposing the motion?"; no reliable SVG metric.

- **F2.3 Agent animation helps dynamic firms and hurts stable firms.** Brasel, S. A., & Hagtvedt, H. (2016). Living brands: consumer responses to animated brand logos. *Journal of the Academy of Marketing Science, 44*(5), 639–653. DOI 10.1007/s11747-015-0449-2 (title, journal and abstract verified on the Springer page; OpenAlex lists the first author as "Bräsel", so the first author's given name is not verified). Design: pilot plus five studies, Study 5 with choice behaviour and established brands. Finding per abstract: "As compared with object-animated or static logos, agent animation encourages more favorable attitudes toward dynamic firms but less favorable attitudes toward stable firms"; attitudes improve when the personality suggested by the animation is consistent with slogans or the logo graphic. Grade: moderate. — [abstract](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11747-015-0449-2)
  - Rule: animate the logo as a self-moving agent only for brands positioned as dynamic; keep animation consistent with slogan and graphic.
  - Check: for animated SVG/Lottie, review whether motion reads as self-propelled (agent) or externally moved (object); brief field "dynamic vs stable firm".

- **F2.4 Animated logos: upward preferred; up-right and convex paths for innovative firms.** Guido, G., Pichierri, M., Nataraajan, R., & Pino, G. (2016). Animated logos in mobile marketing communications: The roles of logo movement directions and trajectories. *Journal of Business Research, 69*(12), 6048–6057. DOI 10.1016/j.jbusres.2016.06.003. Design: three experiments (Ns not in abstract). Finding per abstract: "consumers prefer logos moving upward over those moving downward regardless of the company's power and innovativeness"; for highly innovative companies, up-right preferred over up-left, and a convex up-right trajectory preferred over concave or linear. Grade: moderate (single paper). — [abstract](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jbrese/v69y2016i12p6048-6057.html)
  - Rule: logo entrance animations should travel upward; for innovation-positioned brands, up and to the right on a convex path.
  - Check: parse the animation's translate keyframes; assert net Δy < 0 (screen coordinates) and, if "innovative", net Δx > 0.

- **F2.5 Dynamism raises both taste and risk perceptions; regulatory focus moderates.** Li, S., Liu, P., & Zhou, R. (2020). Diagonal or vertical? An empirical study of the impact of food brand logo orientation on consumers' food perception and food attitude. *Food Quality and Preference, 86*, 103985. DOI 10.1016/j.foodqual.2020.103985. Design: four studies; Study 4 with native English speakers (earlier samples' country not stated in abstract; Ns not in abstract). Finding per abstract: "diagonal (vs. vertical) food brand logos result in higher (lower) taste and risk perception"; perceived movement mediates; promotion-focused consumers favour diagonal, prevention-focused favour vertical. Grade: moderate. — [record/abstract](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.foodqual.2020.103985)
  - Rule: diagonal/tilted marks for indulgent, promotion-framed products; upright marks when safety and risk reduction are the purchase motive.
  - Check: SVG — compute the principal-axis angle of the mark or the wordmark baseline angle; flag |angle| between roughly 10° and 80° as "diagonal" (threshold is my suggestion, not from the paper).

- **F2.6 Facing direction interacts with positioning, but only for static-looking logos.** Tang, Y., Wang, L., & Chan, E. Y. (2025). Do you have the correct brand logo? Role of logo direction, perceived logo movement imagery and brand positioning. *Journal of Product & Brand Management, 34*(6), 802–816. DOI 10.1108/JPBM-11-2023-4840. Design: five experiments, N = 1,914, China. Finding per abstract: for modern positioning, static logos facing right (vs left) generate higher purchase intention; for traditional positioning, left-facing static logos are evaluated more favourably; both effects disappear for dynamic logos; brand engagement mediates. No effect sizes in abstract. Grade: moderate (single paper, one country, left-to-right readers not discussed in abstract). — [record/abstract](https://doi.org/10.1108/jpbm-11-2023-4840)
  - Rule: directional symbols (animals, vehicles, arrows, profiles) should face right for modern/forward-looking brands and may face left for heritage brands.
  - Check: detect facing by horizontal asymmetry of mass or by VLM label ("faces left/right"); cross-check with brief positioning. Reading-direction of the market should be a brief field (see F5.6).

- **F2.7 Logo dynamism and market performance; arousal (pointers, findings UNVERIFIED).** Wang, X., Liu, H., & Chen, H. (2023). The impacts of logo dynamism and product types on brand market performance. *Journal of Business Research, 166*, 114060. DOI 10.1016/j.jbusres.2023.114060; Fennell, P. B., & Schneider, G. (2023). The influence of visually dynamic imagery on purchase intentions: The roles of arousal and lay rationalism. *Journal of Retailing and Consumer Services, 75*, 103537. DOI 10.1016/j.jretconser.2023.103537. Only bibliographic records verified; abstracts were not retrievable. — [Wang et al. record](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jbusres.2023.114060); [Fennell & Schneider record](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jretconser.2023.103537)

- **F2.8 Unstable-looking logos shift inferences to the environment for safety products.** Rahinel, R., & Nelson, N. M. (2016). When Brand Logos Describe the Environment: Design Instability and the Utility of Safety-Oriented Products. *Journal of Consumer Research, 43*(3), 478–496. DOI 10.1093/jcr/ucw039. Design: five experiments (Ns not in abstract). Finding per abstract: with safety-oriented products, consumers exposed to unstable-looking logos "infer the presence of unsafe conditions", apply that to the environment, and this "increases the perceived utility of safety-oriented products". Grade: moderate (counter-intuitive, single paper, no replication found). — [record/abstract](https://doi.org/10.1093/jcr/ucw039)
  - Rule: do not assume instability always harms safety brands; test it. Default remains "match stability to concept" (F2.9).
  - Check: stability proxy — horizontal distance between the mark's centroid and the midpoint of its base of support, normalised by width; tilt angle.

- **F2.9 Stability fits conservation values, instability fits openness values.** Huang, H., Cai, Y., & Xu, L. (2021). Stability or instability: the impact of brand concepts on logo design preferences. *Journal of Brand Management, 28*(5), 465–480. DOI 10.1057/s41262-021-00238-6. Design: three studies. Finding per abstract: consumers "prefer a stable logo brand when it embodies conservation concepts" and "an unstable logo brand when it embodies openness concepts"; mechanism processing fluency; attenuated for people low in visual information processing. Grade: moderate. — [abstract](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1057/s41262-021-00238-6)
  - Rule: match visual stability to the brand's value concept (tradition/security → stable; exploration/novelty → unstable).
  - Check: same stability proxy as F2.8, compared with a brief field for brand values.

### Inferences
- Across F2.1, F2.3, F2.5, F2.6, F2.9 the consistent pattern is a fit effect: dynamism/instability is not good or bad in itself, it must match positioning (dynamic/modern/promotion/openness vs stable/traditional/prevention/conservation). A design system should therefore encode dynamism as a parameter keyed to the brief, not as a universal score.
- The mechanism is contested between engagement (Cian et al.), work/energy (Baxter & Ilicic) and fluency of fit (Brasel & Hagtvedt; Huang et al.).

### Gaps
- No per-study N, country or effect size for Cian et al., Baxter & Ilicic, Brasel & Hagtvedt, Guido et al. (paywalled).
- No direct replication of the Cian et al. eye-tracking result was found.

## 3. Incompleteness / closure, spacing and white space (Hagtvedt 2011 and related)

### Takeaway
Incomplete typeface logos trade trustworthiness for innovativeness and lower overall attitude only among prevention-focused consumers. Related spacing findings show that spaciousness inside a text logo can reduce perceived safety, while space between elements of pictorial logos can improve evaluations.

### Cited Findings

- **F3.1 Incomplete typeface: less trustworthy, more innovative.** Hagtvedt, H. (2011). The Impact of Incomplete Typeface Logos on Perceptions of the Firm. *Journal of Marketing, 75*(4), 86–93. DOI 10.1509/jmkg.75.4.86. Design: three studies (Ns and country not in abstract). Finding per abstract: incomplete typeface logos "have an unfavorable influence on perceived firm trustworthiness" (tied to perceived clarity) and "a favorable influence on perceived firm innovativeness" (tied to perceived interestingness); they "have an unfavorable influence on overall attitude toward the firm, but only for consumers with a prevention, rather than promotion, focus". No effect sizes in abstract. Grade: moderate (multi-study single paper; conceptually echoed by F4.3 on broken frames). — [record/abstract](https://doi.org/10.1509/jmkg.75.4.86)
  - Rule: use cut, stencilled or partially erased letterforms only when innovativeness is the priority and trust/safety is not (avoid for finance, health, security, insurance; acceptable for creative/tech challengers).
  - Check: SVG — compare each glyph outline to the reference glyph of the same font (area ratio or Hausdorff distance); count disconnected sub-paths per letter; flag letters whose contour is open or whose area is below roughly 85–90% of the reference (threshold is my suggestion). If the wordmark is outlined, OCR the rasterized logo and treat a recognition-confidence drop as an incompleteness/clarity signal.

- **F3.2 Interstitial space in text logos lowers safety perceptions.** Gupta, T., & Hagtvedt, H. (2021). Safe Together, Vulnerable Apart: How Interstitial Space in Text Logos Impacts Brand Attitudes in Tight versus Loose Cultures. *Journal of Consumer Research, 48*(3), 474–491. DOI 10.1093/jcr/ucab006. Design: five studies including lab and field experiments and archival analysis (Ns not in abstract). Finding per abstract: "spacious (vs. compact) arrangement of letters—unfavorably influences brand attitude by reducing product safety perceptions"; when threats are salient the effect occurs in tight but not loose cultures; when threats are not salient it "appears to occur across cultures". Grade: moderate. — [record/abstract](https://doi.org/10.1093/jcr/ucab006)
  - Rule: keep letterspacing compact for brands where product safety matters; wide tracking is a risk, particularly in tight cultures and threat contexts.
  - Check: SVG — mean gap between adjacent glyph bounding boxes divided by cap height (or the `letter-spacing` attribute over font size); report as a tracking ratio and compare against a brand-category threshold set by testing.

- **F3.3 Active white space between elements of pictorial logos improves evaluation.** Sharma, N., & Varki, S. (2018). Active White Space (AWS) in Logo Designs: Effects on Logo Evaluations and Brand Communication. *Journal of Advertising, 47*(3), 270–281. DOI 10.1080/00913367.2018.1463880. Design: three studies (Ns not in abstract). Finding per abstract: "adding AWS to pictorial logos improves their visual evaluation"; logos with AWS "are perceived to communicate brand descriptions more clearly"; benefits are largest for sophisticated brand personalities, then sincere, exciting and competent, "with no effect on rugged brand personalities". Grade: moderate. — [record/abstract](https://doi.org/10.1080/00913367.2018.1463880)
  - Rule: separate the elements of a pictorial mark with deliberate negative space, especially for sophisticated brands; do not expect a benefit for rugged brands.
  - Check: SVG — ratio of background area inside the mark's convex hull to hull area; minimum gap between distinct filled shapes relative to stroke weight.

- **F3.4 Spacing and complexity change fluency under repetition.** Janiszewski, C., & Meyvis, T. (2001). Effects of Brand Logo Complexity, Repetition, and Spacing on Processing Fluency and Judgment. *Journal of Consumer Research, 28*(1), 18–32. DOI 10.1086/321945. Design: four studies testing two-factor vs dual-process accounts of fluency. The abstract states the conceptual claim ("stimulus characteristics and presentation factors will interact with repetition to determine the amount of processing fluency") but not the direction of results. Grade: moderate; direction of effects UNVERIFIED from abstract. — [record/abstract](https://doi.org/10.1086/321945)

### Inferences
- F3.2 and F3.3 look contradictory but concern different objects: spacing between letters of a wordmark (reduces safety) vs spacing between elements of a pictorial mark (improves evaluation). A checker should compute the two metrics separately.
- Hagtvedt's trade-off (clarity → trust; interestingness → innovativeness) suggests measuring both perceived clarity and perceived interestingness at concept testing, rather than a single liking score.

### Gaps
- Sample sizes and effect sizes for Hagtvedt (2011) are not in the abstract; full text was paywalled.
- No study was found that tests incompleteness in symbols (as opposed to typeface) or Gestalt closure marks specifically.

## 4. Frames and containers (Fajardo, Zhang & Tsiros 2016 and related)

### Takeaway
A frame around a logo reads as protection when purchase risk is high and as confinement when risk is low, moving purchase intent in opposite directions; later papers extend this to food type (utilitarian vs hedonic) and to brand breadth/extensions.

### Cited Findings

- **F4.1 Frame = protection under high risk, confinement under low risk.** Fajardo, T. M., Zhang, J., & Tsiros, M. (2016). The Contingent Nature of the Symbolic Associations of Visual Design Elements: The Case of Brand Logo Frames. *Journal of Consumer Research, 43*(4), 549–566. DOI 10.1093/jcr/ucw048. Design: six studies (Ns and country not in abstract). Finding per abstract: "A high perception of risk increases the accessibility of a logo frame's association with protection and increases purchase intent, whereas a low perception of risk increases the accessibility of a logo frame's association with confinement and decreases purchase intent"; the effect may extend to secondary brand identifiers such as slogans but not to elements unrelated to brand identity. No effect sizes in abstract. Grade: moderate, conceptually replicated by F4.2 in another lab and domain. — [record/abstract](https://doi.org/10.1093/jcr/ucw048)
  - Rule: enclose the logo (shield, box, roundel) for high-risk purchases; leave it unframed for low-risk, freedom/enjoyment purchases.
  - Check: SVG — detect a closed path/rect/circle whose bounding box contains all other elements and whose fill or stroke forms an enclosure; output framed / partially framed (stroke with gaps) / unframed. Brief field: perceived purchase risk (financial, physical, performance).

- **F4.2 Food brands: framed for utilitarian, unframed for hedonic.** Zou, H., Wang, H., & Li, J. (2023). Framed or Unframed? An empirical study of the impact of food brand logo frame on consumers' food preferences. *Food Research International, 166*, 112602. DOI 10.1016/j.foodres.2023.112602. Design: five studies; Study 5 with UK consumers (other samples' country not in abstract). Finding per abstract: for utilitarian foods framed logos raise preference via food-safety associations; for hedonic foods unframed logos raise preference, with framed logos evoking confinement. Grade: moderate. — [record/abstract](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.foodres.2023.112602)
  - Rule: same as F4.1 with product type as the cue: utilitarian/safety → frame; hedonic → no frame.
  - Check: brief field "utilitarian vs hedonic".

- **F4.3 Removing or breaking the frame broadens the brand; complete frames signal trust to prevention-focused consumers.** Chen, Y.-S. A., & Bei, L.-T. (2019). Free the brand: How a logo frame influences the potentiality of brand extensions. *Journal of Brand Management, 26*(4), 349–364. DOI 10.1057/s41262-018-0142-0 (four experiments); and Chen, Y.-S. A., & Bei, L.-T. (2020). The effects of logo frame design on brand extensions. *Journal of Product & Brand Management, 29*(1), 97–113. DOI 10.1108/JPBM-12-2017-1698 (two experiments; full, partial and open frames). Findings per abstracts: an open logo encourages relational elaboration and participants "associated broader product portfolios with a brand that had an open logo than a framed logo"; removing the frame improved attitudes to a new extension; removing or breaking frames helps "especially for promotion-focused consumers", while "prevention-focused people held favorable brand extension attitudes when the brand logo constructs a complete frame due to its perceived trustworthiness". Grade: moderate (same author team, two papers). — [2019 abstract](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1057/s41262-018-0142-0); [2020 record/abstract](https://doi.org/10.1108/jpbm-12-2017-1698)
  - Rule: brands planning to extend across categories should drop or open the container; single-category trust brands can keep a closed one.
  - Check: brief field "extension plans / brand breadth"; SVG frame-closure ratio = enclosed perimeter covered by stroke/fill ÷ total perimeter (1 = full frame, 0 = none).

### Inferences
- Frames and incompleteness share one moderator family: prevention focus/risk favours closed, complete, compact forms; promotion focus/low risk favours open, broken, spacious forms (F3.1, F3.2, F4.1, F4.3, F2.5). This is a synthesis across papers, not a tested unified theory.
- A responsive identity can carry both a framed and an unframed lockup and deploy them by context (e.g., framed on warranty/payment screens); this is a design inference, untested.

### Gaps
- Sample sizes and effect sizes for Fajardo et al. are not in the abstract.
- No evidence was found on frame shape (circle vs square vs shield) as distinct from frame presence within this literature; shape effects are covered by circular-vs-angular work (Jiang et al. 2016, *JCR* 42(5), 709–726, DOI 10.1093/jcr/ucv049) — [record](https://doi.org/10.1093/jcr/ucv049).

## 5. Position, placement, size and orientation

### Takeaway
Vertical placement carries power/quality meaning (high placement suits powerful brands; one paper finds higher is better regardless of familiarity), upward diagonals signal activity, and logo size effects depend on brand tier, personality, consumption visibility and consumer power. Almost all of this evidence concerns logos on packaging, products or ads, not the internal composition of the mark.

### Cited Findings

- **F5.1 High placement for powerful brands, low for less powerful brands.** Sundar, A., & Noseworthy, T. J. (2014). Place the Logo High or Low? Using Conceptual Metaphors of Power in Packaging Design. *Journal of Marketing, 78*(5), 138–151. DOI 10.1509/jm.13.0253. Design: three studies (Ns and country not in abstract). Finding per abstract: "consumers prefer powerful brands more when the brand logo is featured high rather than low on the brand's packaging, whereas they prefer less powerful brands more when the brand logo is featured low rather than high"; mechanism is "a fluency effect derived from consumers intuitively linking the concept of power with height"; boundary: varying the person's own state of power to be at odds with the metaphor. Grade: moderate; partly contradicted on the moderator by F5.2. — [record/abstract](https://doi.org/10.1509/jm.13.0253)
  - Rule: market leaders place the logo in the upper part of the pack/layout; challengers and underdog brands may place it low.
  - Check: layout metric = (logo centroid y) / (canvas height), with 0 at top; flag > 0.5 for brands the brief marks as category leaders. Applies to lockup-in-layout templates, not to the mark's internal geometry.

- **F5.2 Higher logo location → higher perceived quality → purchase intention, regardless of familiarity.** Dong, R., & Gleim, M. R. (2018). High or low: The impact of brand logo location on consumers product perceptions. *Food Quality and Preference, 69*, 28–35. DOI 10.1016/j.foodqual.2018.05.003. Design: two studies — a Stroop reaction-time task and an online product experiment (Ns not in abstract). Finding per abstract: "consumers evaluate positive words more quickly when they are positioned higher, however there is no significant difference for negative words"; purchase intentions and willingness to recommend are influenced by logo location "due to the indirect effect of perceived quality"; brands that position the logo higher "regardless of brand familiarity, are likely to have more favorable perceptions". Grade: weak-to-moderate (two studies; indirect effect). — [record/abstract](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.foodqual.2018.05.003)
  - Rule: when in doubt, place the logo in the upper half of food/drink packaging.
  - Check: same centroid-y metric as F5.1.

- **F5.3 Upward diagonals convey activity and effort.** Schlosser, A. E., Rikhi, R. R., & Dagogo-Jack, S. W. (2016). The ups and downs of visual orientation: The effects of diagonal orientation on product judgment. *Journal of Consumer Psychology, 26*(4), 496–509. DOI 10.1016/j.jcps.2016.01.003. Design: four experiments plus a content analysis (Ns not in abstract). Finding per abstract: upward (ascending) diagonals convey "greater activity and effort" than downward diagonals; "when the context highlights the benefits of activity (vs. passivity), upward (vs. downward) diagonals lead to more favorable product judgments, greater product efficacy beliefs, and greater post-consumption satisfaction"; efficacy beliefs mediate; "the effect is strongest when the object being visually oriented is text rather than images"; diagonal orientation is "a relatively underutilized design feature". Grade: moderate; converges with F2.5 (diagonal → movement) from another lab. — [record/abstract](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jcps.2016.01.003)
  - Rule: set the wordmark on an ascending baseline (rising left to right) for products sold on activity/efficacy; use level or descending orientation when passivity/relaxation is the benefit.
  - Check: SVG — baseline angle from the `transform="rotate()"` on the text group or from a line fitted through glyph baseline points; ascending in a left-to-right script means the right end is higher (negative rotation in SVG's y-down coordinates). Report sign and degrees.

- **F5.4 Bigger logo fits exciting brands in public consumption.** Cai, Y., & Mo, T. (2020). Making an exciting brand big: Brand personality, logo size and brand evaluation. *Canadian Journal of Administrative Sciences, 37*(3), 259–267. DOI 10.1002/cjas.1545. Design: Studies 1, 2a, 2b (Ns not in abstract). Finding per abstract: "combining a big logo and a high-excitement brand ... leads to high processing fluency, further resulting in more favourable brand evaluation"; weakened in private consumption (T-shirt worn at home) and "insignificant for a private product category (slippers)". Grade: weak-to-moderate. — [record/abstract](https://doi.org/10.1002/cjas.1545)
  - Rule: exciting brands can scale the logo up on publicly consumed products; no gain on private products.
  - Check: logo area ÷ product-face area in mock-ups; brief fields "brand personality" and "public vs private consumption".

- **F5.5 Consumer power × self-construal shape preferred logo size.** Wong, J., Lalwani, A. K., & Wang, J. J. (2022). The interactive effect of power and self-construal on consumers' preferences for brand-logo size. *Journal of Business Research, 150*, 279–296. DOI 10.1016/j.jbusres.2022.05.050. Design: six studies. Finding per abstract: "larger brand logos are preferred by low-power (vs. high-power) consumers when an independent self-construal is salient" (self-deceptive enhancement), while "a smaller brand logo is preferred by low-power (vs. high-power) consumers when an interdependent self-construal is salient" (impression management). Grade: moderate. — [abstract](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jbrese/v150y2022icp279-296.html)
  - Rule: logo size on product is an audience decision (status needs, culture), not a universal "bigger is better".
  - Check: provide size variants in the identity system; brief field for audience and cultural context.

- **F5.6 Status signalling and logo prominence.** Han, Y. J., Nunes, J. C., & Drèze, X. (2010). Signaling Status with Luxury Goods: The Role of Brand Prominence. *Journal of Marketing, 74*(4), 15–30. DOI 10.1509/jmkg.74.4.015. Design: field experiments plus market data including counterfeits. Finding per abstract: wealthy consumers low in need for status "pay a premium for quiet goods only they can recognize"; wealthy consumers high in need for status use "loud luxury goods"; those high in need for status who cannot afford luxury use loud counterfeits. Grade: strong within its domain by citation and follow-up volume (2,141 citations in OpenAlex; many follow-ups on "brand prominence"), though I did not verify a formal replication or meta-analysis. — [record/abstract](https://doi.org/10.1509/jmkg.74.4.015)
  - Rule: top-tier luxury uses small/discreet marks; conspicuous marks serve status-seeking segments.
  - Check: prominence = logo area ÷ product area and contrast against substrate; deliver a "quiet" variant (tone-on-tone, small) and a "loud" variant.

- **F5.7 Field data: logo size in social posts depends on brand tier.** Jo, W., Kim, H., & Choi, J. (2025). A picture's worth a thousand shares: An empirical analysis of logo sizes in social media posts and their impact on content virality. *Marketing Letters, 36*(2), 243–257. DOI 10.1007/s11002-024-09736-4. Design: Twitter API data for 54 major fashion brands over 55 months. Finding per abstract: "larger logos in posted images correspond to a greater number of retweets for midtier fashion brands"; "an opposite effect for top-tier brands—the smaller the logo, the greater the number of retweets"; bottom-tier brands benefit from larger logos, but less than mid-tier. Grade: weak-to-moderate (observational, one category) but consistent with F5.6. — [abstract](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11002-024-09736-4)
  - Rule: mid-tier brands show the logo larger in social imagery; top-tier brands show it smaller.
  - Check: logo bounding-box area ÷ image area in social templates.

- **F5.8 In print ads, the brand element transfers attention; enlarging it does not raise total attention.** Pieters, R., & Wedel, M. (2004). Attention Capture and Transfer in Advertising: Brand, Pictorial, and Text-Size Effects. *Journal of Marketing, 68*(2), 36–50. DOI 10.1509/jmkg.68.2.36.27794. Design: 1,363 print ads eye-tracked on more than 3,600 consumers. Finding per abstract: "The brand element most effectively transfers attention to the other elements. Only increments in the text element's surface size produce a net gain in attention to the advertisement as a whole." Grade: strong for its claim on sample size alone (very large eye-tracking dataset), single paper. — [record/abstract](https://doi.org/10.1509/jmkg.68.2.36.27794)
  - Rule: do not enlarge the logo in an ad expecting more total attention; its job is to route attention.
  - Check: none at the SVG level; layout-level area shares of brand, pictorial and text.

- **F5.9 Left–right: horizontal position and time metaphors (product images, not logos).** Chae, B., & Hoegg, J. (2013). The Future Looks "Right": Effects of the Horizontal Location of Advertising Images on Product Attitude. *Journal of Consumer Research, 40*(2), 223–238. DOI 10.1086/669476. Design: four studies. Finding per abstract: left-to-right readers visualise past on the left and future on the right; product images placed congruently yield more favourable attitudes; "The effect of horizontal position reverses among consumers who read from right to left"; fluency mediates, need for structure moderates. Grade: moderate; application to logos is an extrapolation (see F2.6 for a logo-specific direction result). — [record/abstract](https://doi.org/10.1086/669476)
  - Rule: in left-to-right markets, forward/future cues point or sit right; mirror for right-to-left markets.
  - Check: brief field "script direction of primary market"; facing-direction detection as in F2.6.

- **F5.10 Proportion.** Pittard, N., Ewing, M., & Jevons, C. (2007). Aesthetic theory and logo design: examining consumer response to proportion across cultures. *International Marketing Review, 24*(4), 457–473. DOI 10.1108/02651330710761026. Design: survey in Australia, Singapore and South Africa (Ns not in abstract). Finding per abstract: "universal preference for the divine proportion across cultures"; natural-form logos in the divine proportion (1:1.618) were most preferred, "but for artificially constructed logos, a 1:1 ratio was preferred". Authors note a limited set of ratios was tested. Grade: weak (single survey, limited ratios; golden-ratio preference is a classically contested claim in aesthetics generally). — [record/abstract](https://doi.org/10.1108/02651330710761026)
  - Rule: abstract/geometric marks can default to a 1:1 bounding box; organic figurative marks tolerate about 1.6:1.
  - Check: SVG — bounding-box aspect ratio of the symbol (width ÷ height).

- **F5.11 Luxury logos on clothing change how the wearer is treated.** Lee, J., Ko, E., & Megehee, C. M. (2015). Social benefits of brand logos in presentation of self in cross and same gender influence contexts. *Journal of Business Research, 68*(6), 1341–1349. DOI 10.1016/j.jbusres.2014.12.004. Design: three experiments. Finding per abstract: luxury logos "associate positively with displayer wealth and status"; wearers receive preferential treatment and larger charitable contributions. Grade: weak-to-moderate; about logo presence, not design. — [abstract](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jbrese/v68y2015i6p1341-1349.html)

### Inferences
- F5.1 and F5.2 agree that height is positive for strong/known brands and disagree for weak brands (Sundar & Noseworthy: low is better for less powerful brands; Dong & Gleim: higher is better regardless of familiarity). Note the moderators differ (brand power vs brand familiarity), so this is a partial, not a clean, contradiction.
- Size results (F5.4–F5.7) converge on an inverted status logic: the higher the brand tier or the consumer's secure status, the smaller the optimal mark.
- Orientation findings are strongest for text (F5.3), so the measurable target in an SVG is the wordmark baseline angle rather than the symbol.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed evidence was retrieved on logo position within websites/apps (e.g., left vs centre header); practitioner usability studies exist but were out of scope and not verified.
- A Chinese-language paper on upright vs inclined logo shapes was seen only as a title (Foreign Economics & Management, 2019, DOI 10.16538/j.cnki.fem.2019.02.007); findings UNVERIFIED — [record](https://doi.org/10.16538/j.cnki.fem.2019.02.007).
- Sample sizes and effect sizes for Sundar & Noseworthy and Schlosser et al. are not in the abstracts.

## 6. Mark type: wordmark vs symbol vs combination; with or without brand name

### Takeaway
There is no blanket "symbols beat wordmarks" result. Park et al. (2013) asks which of names or visual symbols better deliver self-expressive, functional and aesthetic benefits; Morgan et al. (2021) show the answer flips with brand familiarity (image-based for familiar brands, text-based for unfamiliar ones); for brand-new marks, icon + name is rated more attractive than either alone.

### Cited Findings

- **F6.1 Logo benefits (self-expressiveness, functional benefit, aesthetic appeal) drive commitment and performance.** Park, C. W., Eisingerich, A. B., Pol, G., & Park, J. W. (2013). The role of brand logos in firm performance. *Journal of Business Research, 66*(2), 180–187. DOI 10.1016/j.jbusres.2012.07.011. Finding per abstract: "the positive effects of brand logos on customer brand commitment and firm performance derive not from enabling brand identification, as is currently understood, but primarily from facilitating customer self-identity/expressiveness, representing a brand's functional benefits, and offering aesthetic appeal"; the study "examines whether brand names or visual symbols as logos are more effective at creating these benefits" and whether effects depend on brand leveraging (extensions). The abstract does NOT state which mark type won, nor N, country or effect sizes. The commonly repeated claim that separate visual symbols outperform name-only logos in this paper is UNVERIFIED here (full text blocked). Grade: weak-to-moderate (single paper; survey/secondary design details unverified). — [abstract](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jbrese/v66y2013i2p180-187.html); [record](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jbusres.2012.07.011)
  - Rule: evaluate concepts on three benefits — does it let customers express identity, does it represent a functional benefit, is it aesthetically appealing — rather than on identification alone.
  - Check: three-item concept test per benefit with target customers; no SVG metric.

- **F6.2 Brand familiarity decides image-based vs text-based.** Morgan, C., Fajardo, T. M., & Townsend, C. (2021). Show it or say it: how brand familiarity influences the effectiveness of image-based versus text-based logos. *Journal of the Academy of Marketing Science, 49*(3), 566–583. DOI 10.1007/s11747-020-00760-0. Design: four studies — secondary data on "several hundred global brands with logos of varying image-to-text design ratios" plus three controlled experiments. Finding per abstract: "when consumers are familiar with a brand, they respond more favorably to image-based logos than to text-based logos. However, when less familiar, consumers prefer text-based logos over image-based logos"; mechanism processing fluency; "a novel moderator to the common empirical finding that images are favorable to text". Grade: moderate. — [abstract](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11747-020-00760-0)
  - Rule: launch with a text-dominant logo; shift weight toward the symbol as familiarity grows (plan the lockup so the symbol can later stand alone).
  - Check: SVG — image-to-text ratio = area of non-text paths ÷ area of text/outlined-glyph paths (the paper's secondary analysis uses image-to-text design ratios); requires tagging which paths are glyphs (keep live `<text>` or a `data-role="wordmark"` group in source files).

- **F6.3 For unknown brands, icon + name beats either alone; black beats coloured.** Bresciani, S., & Del Ponte, P. (2017). New brand logo design: customers' preference for brand name and icon. *Journal of Brand Management, 24*(5), 375–390. DOI 10.1057/s41262-017-0046-4. Design: multiple picture sorting with 93 subjects on unknown logos, then experiments (experiment Ns and country not in abstract). Finding per abstract: "logos composed of an icon plus a brand name are perceived as significantly more attractive than logos made of one component only, and that black logos are perceived as more attractive than coloured logos". Grade: weak-to-moderate (attractiveness only). — [abstract](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1057/s41262-017-0046-4)
  - Rule: a start-up's primary lockup should be a combination mark.
  - Check: SVG — assert both a symbol group and a wordmark group exist in the primary lockup.

- **F6.4 Showing the company name increases the associations a new symbol evokes.** van Riel, C. B. M., & van den Ban, A. (2001). The added value of corporate logos – An empirical study. *European Journal of Marketing, 35*(3/4), 428–440. DOI 10.1108/03090560110382093. Design: before/after evaluation of a new Dutch bank logo against two competitors' logos (N not in abstract; Netherlands). Finding per abstract: associations "appeared to increase if respondents were confronted with the name of the company behind the new logo"; after launch with a national campaign, positive associations increased and negative ones decreased; most interviewees described associations matching the bank's intentions. Grade: weak (single case). — [record/abstract](https://doi.org/10.1108/03090560110382093)
  - Rule: do not release a new symbol without the name until the association is learned.

- **F6.5 Memory: pictures over names in fast contexts, but names cue logos better than logos cue names.** Ghosh, T., Sreejesh, S., & Dwivedi, Y. K. (2022). Brand logos versus brand names: A comparison of the memory effects of textual and pictorial brand elements placed in computer games. *Journal of Business Research, 147*, 222–235. DOI 10.1016/j.jbusres.2022.04.017 — per abstract, logos lead to stronger memory than names (picture superiority), neutralised when game speed is reduced or when physical distinctiveness of the elements is increased. Dommer, S. L., & Parker, J. R. (2023). Cued-recall asymmetries: the case of brand names and logos. *Marketing Letters, 34*(4), 669–684. DOI 10.1007/s11002-023-09697-0 — per abstract, for newly encountered brands "consumers are more likely to accurately recall a newly-encountered brand's logo when cued by its name than the converse", not an artifact of logos being easier to recall. Grade: moderate for each (multi-study), different paradigms. — [Ghosh et al.](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jbusres.2022.04.017); [Dommer & Parker](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11002-023-09697-0)
  - Rule: a symbol alone will not reliably retrieve the name of a new brand; keep the name in the lockup wherever name recall matters (search, word of mouth).
  - Check: unaided name recall from symbol-only exposure as a launch-readiness test.

- **F6.6 Corporate name, design and typeface are antecedents of logo favourability.** Foroudi, P., Melewar, T. C., & Gupta, S. (2014). Linking corporate logo, corporate image, and reputation: An examination of consumer perceptions in the financial setting. *Journal of Business Research, 67*(11), 2269–2281. DOI 10.1016/j.jbusres.2014.06.015. Finding per abstract: main influences on a favourable corporate logo are "corporate name, design, and typeface"; a favourable logo enhances corporate image, attitude toward advertisements, recognizability, familiarity and reputation. N and country not in abstract. Grade: weak-to-moderate (survey/SEM, one sector). — [abstract](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jbrese/v67y2014i11p2269-2281.html)

- **F6.7 Co-brand logos: integrative beats added or novel.** Kraus, A., & Gierl, H. (2017). The logo matters: the effect of the logo type on the attitude towards co-products. *International Journal of Advertising, 36*(5), 743–760. DOI 10.1080/02650487.2017.1340926. Finding per abstract: an integrative logo (composed of elements of both originals) yields "more favourable perceptions of brand cooperation, higher perceptions of co-product quality, more positive feelings and a more favourable attitude" than added (side-by-side) or novel logos; novel logos caused "irritation and frustration". Grade: weak-to-moderate. — [record/abstract](https://doi.org/10.1080/02650487.2017.1340926)
  - Rule: co-brand marks should fuse recognisable elements of both parents.

- **F6.8 Descriptiveness does not depend on mark type.** Luffarelli et al. (2019), Studies 3 and 6: no evidence that logo type (wordmark vs icon-only vs mixed) moderates the descriptiveness effect. — [accepted manuscript](https://sussex.figshare.com/articles/journal_contribution/Let_the_logo_do_the_talking_the_influence_of_logo_descriptiveness_on_brand_equity/23483705)

- **F6.9 Physimorphic vs typographic logos (pointer; findings UNVERIFIED).** Roy, S., & Attri, R. (2022). Physimorphic vs. Typographic logos in destination marketing: Integrating destination familiarity and consumer characteristics. *Tourism Management, 92*, 104544. DOI 10.1016/j.tourman.2022.104544. Record verified; abstract not retrievable. — [record](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tourman.2022.104544)

### Inferences
- F6.2 (text for unfamiliar), F6.3 (combination for new brands) and F6.5 (names cue logos, not the reverse) agree on a staged strategy: name-forward at launch, symbol-forward at maturity. This reconciles them with picture-superiority findings, which were obtained under speed/low-attention conditions.
- The claim "symbols outperform wordmarks" should be reported as conditional on familiarity and on the benefit sought (self-expression, function, aesthetics), not as a main effect.

### Gaps
- Park et al. (2013): I could not verify the sample, the method, or the direction of the names-vs-symbols comparison. This is the most important unverified item in this file; the full text (Elsevier) returned a human-verification wall.
- No study was found comparing monograms/lettermarks, emblems and mascots as separate categories against each other.

## 7. Stylized, anthropomorphic, cute, mascot and handmade/handwritten cues

### Takeaway
Human-like and cute cues can raise affect, perceived growth potential, perceived functional performance and forgiveness, but effects are conditional (emotion state, repeated transgressions, brand experience, functional positioning) and most logo-specific papers are single-team or single-study.

### Cited Findings

- **F7.1 Personality ascriptions to logos: activeness helps, aggressiveness hurts.** Payne, C. R., Hyman, M. R., Niculescu, M., & Huhmann, B. A. (2013). Anthropomorphic responses to new-to-market logos. *Journal of Marketing Management, 29*(1–2), 122–140. DOI 10.1080/0267257X.2013.770413. Design: 120 US collegiate sports logos rated by three logo experts and 119 consumers. Finding per abstract: "processing fluency mediates the relationship between elaborateness and all logo personality dimensions"; "ascriptions of aggressiveness (negatively) and activeness (positively) influence consumer affect and purchase intentions". Grade: weak-to-moderate (one sample, sports logos). — [record/abstract](https://doi.org/10.1080/0267257x.2013.770413)
  - Rule: mascot/character marks should read as active but not aggressive.
  - Check: human or VLM ratings of "active" and "aggressive"; feature proxies for aggression (bared teeth, angled brows, sharp angles) are my suggestion, not from the paper.

- **F7.2 Anthropomorphised logo → perceived functional performance.** Daryanto, A., Alexander, N., & Kartika, G. (2022). The anthropomorphic brand logo and its effect on perceived functional performance. *Journal of Brand Management, 29*(3), 287–300. DOI 10.1057/s41262-022-00271-z. Design: survey with a hypothetical anthropomorphised logo, structural equation modelling (N and country not in abstract). Finding per abstract: an anthropomorphised logo representing a culturally embedded iconic character "has a positive impact on perceived functional performance", stronger as logo appeal increases; logo–self connection is the mechanism. Grade: weak (single survey study). — [abstract](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1057/s41262-022-00271-z)
  - Rule: culturally familiar characters can carry performance meaning, provided the character itself is liked.

- **F7.3 Schema congruity governs whether anthropomorphism works (products, not logos).** Aggarwal, P., & McGill, A. L. (2007). Is That Car Smiling at Me? Schema Congruity as a Basis for Evaluating Anthropomorphized Products. *Journal of Consumer Research, 34*(4), 468–479. DOI 10.1086/518544. Design: three studies. Finding per abstract: evaluation depends "on the extent to which that product is endowed with characteristics congruent with the proposed human schema"; perceiving the product as human mediates; "the affective tag attached to the specific human schema moderates the evaluation". Grade: strong as a foundation by follow-up volume (1,310 citations in OpenAlex), but it is about products; transfer to logos is an inference. — [record/abstract](https://doi.org/10.1086/518544)
  - Rule: face-like marks need complete, coherent facial configuration (eyes + mouth in plausible positions) and a positive expression.
  - Check: SVG — run a face detector on the rasterized mark; or geometric heuristic: two similar shapes horizontally aligned above a third centred shape.

- **F7.4 Cute logo → better brand attitude when consumers feel hope.** Septianto, F., & Paramita, W. (2021). Cute brand logo enhances favorable brand attitude: The moderating role of hope. *Journal of Retailing and Consumer Services, 63*, 102734. DOI 10.1016/j.jretconser.2021.102734. Design: two experiments (Ns and country not in abstract). Finding per abstract: a cute (vs non-cute) logo increases positive brand attitude "especially among those experiencing the emotion of hope (but not happiness or a neutral emotional state)", driven by perceived growth potential. Grade: weak-to-moderate. — [abstract](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0969698921003003)
  - Rule: cute (baby-schema) marks suit brands and contexts that sell hope and growth (start-ups, education, causes).
  - Check: baby-schema proxies on a character mark — head-to-body ratio, eye size relative to head, roundness of contours (my operationalisation of kindchenschema, not the paper's).

- **F7.5 Cute logo reduces punishment after a transgression, once.** Septianto, F., & Kwon, J. (2022). Too cute to be bad? Cute brand logo reduces consumer punishment following brand transgressions. *International Journal of Research in Marketing, 39*(4), 1108–1126. DOI 10.1016/j.ijresmar.2021.12.006. Design: five experiments. Finding per abstract: high (vs low) cuteness "can motivate consumers to protect the brand from harm, thus reducing consumer punishment"; driven by an incremental belief about the brand's development; "the cuteness effect is attenuated in the case of repeated transgressions". Grade: moderate (same first author as F7.4). — [abstract](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/ijrema/v39y2022i4p1108-1126.html)
  - Rule: cuteness offers a limited goodwill buffer; it does not protect repeat offenders.

- **F7.6 Spokes-characters work through trust and mainly for low-experience consumers.** Garretson, J. A., & Niedrich, R. W. (2004). Spokes-Characters: Creating Character Trust and Positive Brand Attitudes. *Journal of Advertising, 33*(2), 25–36. DOI 10.1080/00913367.2004.10639159. Finding per abstract: "spokes-character trust is an important mediator of the effects of spokes-character features on brand attitude"; features produce more favourable attitudes "for consumers with less brand experience", with "little effect" for experienced consumers. N not in abstract. Grade: moderate. — [record/abstract](https://doi.org/10.1080/00913367.2004.10639159)
  - Rule: mascots pay off most in acquisition (new customers), least with existing customers.
  - Check: measure character trust in concept tests; brief field "acquisition vs retention priority".

- **F7.7 Handwritten typefaces humanise, except for functional products and already-loved brands.** Schroll, R., Schnurr, B., & Grewal, D. (2018). Humanizing Products with Handwritten Typefaces. *Journal of Consumer Research* (DOI 10.1093/jcr/ucy014; volume and pages not returned by the record). Design: several laboratory and field studies. Finding per abstract: handwritten typefaces "create perceptions of human presence, which lead to more favorable product evaluations (and behavior) by enhancing the emotional attachment"; effects "are mitigated for brands to which consumers already feel a sense of attachment" and "reverse when the products are functionally positioned or functional in nature". Grade: moderate; studied on packaging typefaces, so application to logotypes is a near extrapolation. — [record/abstract](https://doi.org/10.1093/jcr/ucy014)
  - Rule: script/handwritten wordmarks for hedonic, human-touch products of new brands; avoid for functional/technical products.
  - Check: SVG — stroke-width variance along glyphs, baseline jitter, connected letterforms; or font-classification of the wordmark into handwritten vs machine.

- **F7.8 Handmade production cue increases attractiveness via "love" (stated production mode, not logo style).** Fuchs, C., Schreier, M., & van Osselaer, S. M. J. (2015). The Handmade Effect: What's Love Got to Do with It? *Journal of Marketing, 79*(2), 98–110. DOI 10.1509/jm.14.0018. Design: four studies. Finding per abstract: a positive handmade effect on product attractiveness, driven by perceptions that handmade products "contain love"; stronger for gifts to loved ones and when buying to convey love. Grade: moderate; relevance to hand-drawn logos is an inference. — [record/abstract](https://doi.org/10.1509/jm.14.0018)

### Inferences
- Anthropomorphic, cute and handwritten cues all appear to work by adding warmth/human presence, and all weaken for consumers who already know or are attached to the brand (F7.6, F7.7) — the same familiarity boundary seen for descriptiveness (F1.3).
- None of the logo-specific papers manipulates facial features inside a logo parametrically; the SVG checks proposed here are my operationalisations and would need validation.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed study specifically on pareidolic/face-like abstract logos was retrieved.
- A meta-analysis of brand anthropomorphism may exist but was not retrieved or verified; none specific to logos was found.
- Volume/issue/pages for Schroll et al. (2018) were not returned by the record and are left blank rather than supplied from memory.

## 8. Name–logo–product congruence and sound symbolism

### Takeaway
Congruence among name sound, mark shape/size/colour and typeface helps communicate intended meaning and is linked to more positive emotional associations; the logo-specific evidence base is small (two older experiments and one 80-person rating study), though it rests on a large and well-replicated psycholinguistic literature on sound–shape correspondences.

### Cited Findings

- **F8.1 Name phonetics map onto mark size, shape and colour; consistent marks communicate meaning better.** Klink, R. R. (2003). Creating Meaningful Brands: The Relationship Between Brand Name and Brand Mark. *Marketing Letters, 14*(3), 143–157. DOI 10.1023/A:1027476132607. Design: two studies (Ns not in abstract). Finding per abstract: Study 1 supports "a relationship between structural characteristics of the brand name (e.g., front vs. back vowels) and the brand mark's size, shape, and color"; Study 2 "found that brands with marks that are consistent in design with the brand name better communicate intended brand meaning". Grade: weak-to-moderate (two studies, 2003, no direct replication found). — [abstract](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1023/A:1027476132607)
  - Rule: match the mark to the name's sound — front vowels/"sharp" sounds with smaller, lighter, more angular marks; back vowels/"round" sounds with larger, darker, rounder marks. (The direction of each mapping is the standard sound-symbolism pattern and is my gloss; the abstract confirms only that the relationship exists.)
  - Check: brief-to-concept — compute a phonetic profile of the name (share of front vs back vowels; voiceless stops/fricatives vs sonorants); SVG — curvature profile (share of path length in curves vs straight segments, count of corners below a given angle) and compare the two profiles.

- **F8.2 Symbol–typeface congruence relates to positive emotions.** Salgado-Montejo, A., Velasco, C., Olier, J. S., Alvarado, J., & Spence, C. (2014). Love for logos: Evaluating the congruency between brand symbols and typefaces and their relation to emotional words. *Journal of Brand Management, 21*(7–8), 635–649. DOI 10.1057/bm.2014.29. Design: nine brand logos split into symbol and typeface; 80 participants (half rated each component) on shape-symbolism scales, plus choice among 20 emotions. Finding per abstract: "brands with more congruent scores also presented more positive as compared with negative emotions". Grade: weak (9 logos, correlational, real brands confounded with prior attitudes). — [record/abstract](https://doi.org/10.1057/bm.2014.29)
  - Rule: keep symbol and typeface on the same side of the round–angular continuum.
  - Check: SVG — compute the curvature/angularity score separately for the symbol group and the wordmark group and report the difference.

- **F8.3 Font–product and colour–product congruity (pointers).** Doyle, J. R., & Bottomley, P. A. (2006). Dressed for the Occasion: Font-Product Congruity in the Perception of Logotype. *Journal of Consumer Psychology, 16*(2), DOI 10.1207/s15327663jcp1602_2; Bottomley, P. A., & Doyle, J. R. (2006). The interactive effects of colors and products on perceptions of brand logo appropriateness. *Marketing Theory*, DOI 10.1177/1470593106061263. Records verified by title/authors/journal/DOI only; findings not extracted here (typography and colour belong to adjacent research scopes). — [Doyle & Bottomley](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15327663jcp1602_2); [Bottomley & Doyle](https://doi.org/10.1177/1470593106061263)

- **F8.4 Sound symbolism in brand names (foundations; records verified, abstracts not retrieved).** Klink, R. R. (2000). Creating Brand Names With Meaning: The Use of Sound Symbolism. *Marketing Letters, 11*(1), 5–20. DOI 10.1023/A:1008184423824; Shrum, L. J., Lowrey, T. M., Luna, D., Lerman, D. B., & Liu, M. (2012). Sound symbolism effects across languages: Implications for global brand names. *International Journal of Research in Marketing, 29*(3), 275–279. DOI 10.1016/j.ijresmar.2012.03.002. Findings UNVERIFIED in this pass. — [Klink 2000](https://doi.org/10.1023/a:1008184423824); [Shrum et al. 2012](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijresmar.2012.03.002)

- **F8.5 Asymmetry–excitement congruence, independently replicated.** Luffarelli, J., Stamatogiannakis, A., & Yang, H. (2019). The Visual Asymmetry Effect: An Interplay of Logo Design and Brand Personality on Brand Equity. *Journal of Marketing Research, 56*(1), 89–103. DOI 10.1177/0022243718820548 — five studies incl. secondary data; asymmetrical logos are more arousing, perceived as more exciting, and boost evaluations and financial valuations of brands with an exciting personality; the interplay occurs "only for the personality of excitement and the visual property of asymmetry". Bajaj, A., & Bond, S. D. (2018). Beyond Beauty: Design Symmetry and Brand Personality. *Journal of Consumer Psychology, 28*(1), 77–98. DOI 10.1002/jcpy.1009 — four experiments; "asymmetry in visual brand elements is associated by consumers with brand excitement", driven partly by subjective arousal. Bettels, J., & Wiedmann, K.-P. (2019). Brand logo symmetry and product design: The spillover effects on consumer inferences. *Journal of Business Research, 97*, 1–9. DOI 10.1016/j.jbusres.2018.12.039 — consumers "associate asymmetry, not symmetry, with excitement"; logo liking spills over to inferred product aesthetics and symbolism more than functionality. Grade: strong by the brief's "replicated" criterion (three independent teams, same direction), no meta-analysis. — [Luffarelli et al.](https://doi.org/10.1177/0022243718820548); [Bajaj & Bond](https://doi.org/10.1002/jcpy.1009); [Bettels & Wiedmann](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jbusres.2018.12.039)
  - Rule: use asymmetry for brands positioned on excitement; symmetry elsewhere.
  - Check: SVG — rasterize, mirror about the vertical (and horizontal) axis through the centroid, compute pixel overlap (IoU); report a 0–1 symmetry score.

### Inferences
- Congruence is the recurring logic across sections 2, 4, 5 and 8: a property helps when it matches name, typeface, personality or positioning, usually via processing fluency. This is the most defensible general principle to carry into a brief-to-concept workflow: capture positioning variables in the brief and score each concept for match rather than for absolute level.
- The bouba/kiki mapping from name sound to mark shape is well grounded in psycholinguistics, but its consumer-outcome evidence for logos specifically rests mainly on Klink (2003).

### Gaps
- Direction and size of the individual name–mark mappings in Klink (2003) could not be read from the abstract.
- No study was found that tests name–logo sound-symbolic congruence on purchase behaviour or with a preregistered design.

## 9. Logo redesign / change

### Takeaway
The best-supported pattern in this file: the more committed, attached, brand-conscious or aesthetically sensitive the consumer, the worse they react to logo change, and the larger the change the worse the reaction; weakly committed consumers react positively. Repeated exposure and empathetic communication mitigate the damage.

### Cited Findings

- **F9.1 Commitment reverses the response to rounding an angular logo.** Walsh, M. F., Winterich, K. P., & Mittal, V. (2010). Do logo redesigns help or hurt your brand? The role of brand commitment. *Journal of Product & Brand Management, 19*(2), 76–84. DOI 10.1108/10610421011033421. Design: field experiment, 632 respondents, two athletic shoe brands (New Balance, Adidas); country not stated in abstract. Finding per abstract: "The greater the degree of change in the roundedness of a previously angular logo, the more likely it is that strongly committed consumers will evaluate the redesigned logo more negatively"; logo evaluation partially mediates the joint effect on brand attitude; "weakly committed consumers react positively to such changes". No effect sizes in abstract. Grade: strong by the "replicated" criterion — same team replicated in two further field experiments (F9.2), and independent teams converge (F9.3, F9.5, F9.6). — [record/abstract](https://doi.org/10.1108/10610421011033421)
  - Rule: when the customer base is highly committed, change the logo in small steps; reserve large changes for brands that need to recruit weakly committed or new customers.
  - Check: degree-of-change metric between old and new SVG — shape IoU after alignment, Hausdorff/Chamfer distance between outlines, change in curvature (roundedness) score, colour ΔE of dominant fills; brief field "share of highly committed customers".

- **F9.2 Replication with thought-listing and a self-construal moderator.** Walsh, M. F., Winterich, K. P., & Mittal, V. (2011). How re-designing angular logos to be rounded shapes brand attitude: consumer brand commitment and self-construal. *Journal of Consumer Marketing, 28*(6), 438–447. DOI 10.1108/07363761111165958. Design: two field experiments (mall intercepts; undergraduates). Finding per abstract: brand commitment "negatively influences evaluation of inconsistent information (i.e. rounded logo)", increases negative thoughts about the logo; the effect "is attenuated when the inconsistent information – i.e. rounded logo – is congruent with the consumers' self-construal (i.e. interdependent self-construal)". Grade: moderate. — [record/abstract](https://doi.org/10.1108/07363761111165958)
  - Rule: frame the change in terms congruent with the audience's self-view.

- **F9.3 Degree of change and brand consciousness; exposure repairs fluency.** van Grinsven, B., & Das, E. (2015). Processing different degrees of logo change: When higher levels of brand consciousness decrease openness to substantial logo changes. *European Journal of Marketing, 49*(11/12), 1710–1727. DOI 10.1108/EJM-02-2014-0127. Design: Experiment 1 N = 120 (no vs small vs substantial change); Experiment 2 N = 148 (3 degree × 2 exposure: 1 vs 3) with brand consciousness as continuous moderator. Finding per abstract: "Substantial logo changes harmed processing speed of highly brand conscious consumers in particular"; substantial changes decreased processing speed, "which was compensated by repeated exposure"; "only a few exposures are needed to neutralize these effects". DV is logo processing speed, not attitude. Grade: moderate. — [record/abstract](https://doi.org/10.1108/ejm-02-2014-0127)
  - Rule: the recognition cost of a substantial change is temporary; budget for repeated exposure at launch.
  - Check: recognition-speed test (old vs new logo) before and after three exposures.

- **F9.4 Attached consumers resist most; empathetic messaging helps.** Walsh, M. F., Cui, A. P., & MacInnis, D. J. (2019). How to successfully introduce logo redesigns. *Journal of Brand Management, 26*(4), 365–375. DOI 10.1057/s41262-018-0141-1. Finding per abstract: "consumers who are most attached to the brand are least receptive to logo redesigns"; greatest success when the change "is accompanied by empathetic messages that acknowledge consumers' feelings of discomfort". N not in abstract. Grade: moderate. — [abstract](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1057/s41262-018-0141-1)
  - Rule: announce a redesign with a message that acknowledges discomfort, addressed to loyal customers first.

- **F9.5 Radical change: familiarity raises negative surprise, attachment raises positive surprise.** Grobert, J., Cuny, C., & Fornerino, M. (2016). Surprise! We changed the logo. *Journal of Product & Brand Management, 25*(3), 239–246. DOI 10.1108/JPBM-06-2015-0895. Design: 220 students (current students and applicants) after a real university logo change (authors' affiliations are French; sample country not stated in abstract). Finding per abstract: "higher familiarity increases negative surprise, whereas higher attachment enhances positive surprise"; surprise matters for perceived congruence between the new logo and brand values. Grade: weak (single study, one school). Note the tension with F9.1/F9.4, where attachment/commitment predicts more negative reactions. — [record/abstract](https://doi.org/10.1108/jpbm-06-2015-0895)
  - Rule: manage surprise — prepare familiar audiences before reveal.

- **F9.6 Fans: colour change and revolutionary change are worst.** Williams, A. S., & Son, S. (2022). Sport rebranding: the effect of different degrees of sport logo redesign on brand attitude and purchase intention. *International Journal of Sports Marketing and Sponsorship, 23*(1), 155–172. DOI 10.1108/IJSMS-01-2021-0016. Design: 4 (degree of logo change) × 2 (fan identification) experiment, N = 277, UFC logo with three fictitious redesigns. Finding per abstract: "color change and revolutionary change generate the most negative attitudes from both high- and low-identified fans, and fan identification is not a significant factor influencing fan attitudes"; for purchase intention low-identified fans were not significantly influenced. Grade: weak-to-moderate; identification was NOT a significant moderator of attitude, unlike commitment in F9.1. Related: Williams, A. S., Son, S., Walsh, P., & Park, J. (2021). The Influence of Logo Change on Brand Loyalty and the Role of Attitude Toward Rebranding and Logo Evaluation. *Sport Marketing Quarterly, 30*(1), 69–81. DOI 10.32731/SMQ.291.032021.06 — logo evaluation partially mediates logo change → loyalty; attitude toward rebranding matters. — [Williams & Son](https://doi.org/10.1108/ijsms-01-2021-0016); [Williams et al.](https://doi.org/10.32731/smq.291.032021.06)
  - Rule: keep the colour when changing the form; avoid changing both at once.
  - Check: colour ΔE between old and new palette as a separate gate from shape distance.

- **F9.7 Redesign changes perceived modernity.** Müller, B., Kocher, B., & Crettaz, A. (2013). The effects of visual rejuvenation through brand logos. *Journal of Business Research, 66*(1), 82–88. DOI 10.1016/j.jbusres.2011.07.026. Design: experiment with 385 respondents (country not in abstract). Finding per abstract: "logo redesign affects the perception of brand modernity"; model links logo characteristics → logo attitude → brand modernity, brand attitude and "finally, brand loyalty". Grade: weak-to-moderate. — [abstract](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jbrese/v66y2013i1p82-88.html)
  - Rule: a redesign is a valid tool when the measured problem is low perceived modernity.

- **F9.8 Processing of change involves both interest and doubt.** Peterson, M., AlShebil, S., & Bishop, M. (2015). Cognitive and emotional processing of brand logo changes. *Journal of Product & Brand Management, 24*(7), 745–757. DOI 10.1108/JPBM-03-2015-0823. Design: 12 in-depth interviews, then field survey of 406 respondents on two retail brands. Finding per abstract: nine of ten hypotheses supported; "both interest in the logo change as well as doubt about the logo change characterize consumers' processing". Grade: weak-to-moderate. — [record/abstract](https://doi.org/10.1108/jpbm-03-2015-0823)

- **F9.9 Counter-evidence: logo variations processed like the original.** Sääksjärvi, M., van den Hende, E., Mugge, R., & van Peursem, N. (2015). How exposure to logos and logo varieties fosters brand prominence and freshness. *Journal of Product & Brand Management, 24*(7), 736–744. DOI 10.1108/JPBM-06-2014-0648. Design: two experiments. Finding per abstract: consumers "subconsciously process logo varieties ... in a similar way as they subconsciously process the existing logo"; findings "diverge from the findings typically reported in the branding literature that state that consumers resist changes to logos". Grade: weak-to-moderate; applies to slight modifications. — [record/abstract](https://doi.org/10.1108/jpbm-06-2014-0648)
  - Rule: small, playful variations of the logo (flexible identity) are safe and can add freshness.

- **F9.10 Aesthetic sensitivity narrows the latitude of acceptance.** Phillips, B. J., McQuarrie, E. F., & Griffin, W. G. (2014). How Visual Brand Identity Shapes Consumer Response. *Psychology & Marketing, 31*(3), 225–236. DOI 10.1002/mar.20689. Design: qualitative work with art directors and consumers plus a follow-up experiment. Finding per abstract: professionals "had a more narrow latitude of acceptance for changes"; "aesthetically aware consumers were likewise more sensitive to alterations in visual brand identity". Grade: weak-to-moderate. — [record/abstract](https://doi.org/10.1002/mar.20689)
  - Rule: designers over-estimate how much change ordinary consumers notice; design-savvy audiences are the exception.

### Inferences
- Commitment (Walsh 2010, 2011), attachment (Walsh 2019), brand consciousness (van Grinsven & Das) and aesthetic sensitivity (Phillips et al.) are different constructs that all predict lower tolerance for change; identification among sports fans did not (Williams & Son). The safest synthesis: tolerance for change falls with the strength of the existing brand schema in the audience.
- Degree of change is measurable from two SVGs; none of the papers offers a numeric threshold for "small" vs "substantial", so any threshold in a tool must be calibrated empirically.

### Gaps
- No effect sizes are given in any redesign abstract; no long-run sales or market data on redesigns were found in the peer-reviewed sources retrieved.
- Walsh et al. (2010) sample country is not stated in the abstract.

## 10. Which effects are contested, small, or unreplicated?

### Takeaway
No meta-analysis or preregistered direct replication was found for any property in this file; the field consists of multi-study single papers linked by conceptual replications. The most robust regularities are fit/congruence effects and commitment-moderated redesign effects; the weakest are single-study claims (proportion, cuteness × hope, anthropomorphic logos → performance) and several main effects that the original authors themselves report at relaxed significance levels.

### Cited Findings

- **F10.1 The field's own review describes it as fragmented.** Kim, M. J., & Lim, J. H. (2019). A comprehensive review on logo literature: research topics, findings, and future directions. *Journal of Marketing Management, 35*(13–14), 1291–1365. DOI 10.1080/0267257X.2019.1604563. Per abstract: reviews 124 studies published in business journals over 30 years, classified into six topics (theoretical foundations, logo design/redesign, basic logo elements, additional logo elements, outcomes of logo use, practical applications); "the logo literature remains fragmented". It is a narrative review, not a meta-analysis. — [record/abstract](https://doi.org/10.1080/0267257x.2019.1604563)

- **F10.2 Descriptiveness: fragile outside the lab.** In Luffarelli et al. (2019), the real-logo survey effect on authenticity is β = .08, p = .065 with the indirect effect significant only at 93%; the familiarity interaction is p = .096 and its moderated mediation is reported at 90% CIs; the sales association is p = .035 and p = .052 after winsorizing, with cross-sectional data and the authors' own caution. — [accepted manuscript](https://sussex.figshare.com/articles/journal_contribution/Let_the_logo_do_the_talking_the_influence_of_logo_descriptiveness_on_brand_equity/23483705)

- **F10.3 Mechanism of dynamism is disputed.** Cian et al. (2014) attribute the attitude effect to engagement; Baxter & Ilicic (2018) report that brand work and brand energy, "rather than brand engagement", explain attitudinal and behavioural effects. — [Cian et al.](https://doi.org/10.1509/jmr.13.0023); contradicted on mechanism by [Baxter & Ilicic](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijresmar.2018.03.002)

- **F10.4 Logo height: moderator disagreement.** Sundar & Noseworthy (2014) find low placement is preferred for less powerful brands; Dong & Gleim (2018) conclude higher placement is favourable "regardless of brand familiarity". — [Sundar & Noseworthy](https://doi.org/10.1509/jm.13.0253); partly contradicted by [Dong & Gleim](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.foodqual.2018.05.003)

- **F10.5 Image vs text: no main effect.** Morgan et al. (2021) show a familiarity crossover and explicitly present it as a moderator of "the common empirical finding that images are favorable to text"; Bresciani & Del Ponte (2017) find combination marks most attractive for unknown brands; Ghosh et al. (2022) find picture superiority that disappears at lower speed or higher distinctiveness. — [Morgan et al.](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11747-020-00760-0); [Bresciani & Del Ponte](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1057/s41262-017-0046-4); [Ghosh et al.](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jbusres.2022.04.017)

- **F10.6 Naturalness/figurativeness: qualified.** Machado et al. (2015) find natural logos "clearly preferred" to abstract ones; Lencastre et al. (2023) conclude "figurativeness is not a sacrosanct solution" and depends on the name–logo interaction; Henderson & Cote (1998) recommend less natural designs for low-investment (false-recognition) goals. — [Machado et al.](https://doi.org/10.1108/jpbm-05-2014-0609); qualified by [Lencastre et al.](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jbusres.2023.113944) and [Henderson & Cote](https://doi.org/10.1177/002224299806200202)

- **F10.7 Simplicity vs complexity: direction depends on product, goal and exposure.** Bossel, V., Geyskens, K., & Goukens, C. (2019). Facing a trend of brand logo simplicity: The impact of brand logo design on consumption. *Food Quality and Preference, 71*, 129–135. DOI 10.1016/j.foodqual.2018.06.009 — two experiments; people consume more from a product with a simple, flat logo, but "for prestigious food products" the opposite occurs. Mahmood, A., Luffarelli, J., & Mukesh, M. (2019). What's in a logo? The impact of complex visual cues in equity crowdfunding. *Journal of Business Venturing, 34*(1), 41–62. DOI 10.1016/j.jbusvent.2018.09.006 — survey, field study and experiment; more complex logos are harder to process, feel more unique/novel, signal venture innovativeness and "can positively impact backers' funding decisions". Miceli et al. (2014) show the sign of visual complexity flips with exposures (F1.9). — [Bossel et al.](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.foodqual.2018.06.009); [Mahmood et al.](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jbusvent.2018.09.006); [Miceli et al.](https://doi.org/10.1002/mar.20741)
  - Rule: simple marks for everyday, high-frequency brands; allow more complexity for prestige or innovation signalling.
  - Check: SVG complexity = node count, path count, distinct fills, compressed size.

- **F10.8 Redesign resistance is not universal.** Sääksjärvi et al. (2015) report findings that "diverge" from the resistance-to-change literature for slight variations; Williams & Son (2022) find fan identification is not a significant moderator of attitude; Grobert et al. (2016) find attachment increases positive surprise. — [Sääksjärvi et al.](https://doi.org/10.1108/jpbm-06-2014-0648); [Williams & Son](https://doi.org/10.1108/ijsms-01-2021-0016); [Grobert et al.](https://doi.org/10.1108/jpbm-06-2015-0895); versus [Walsh et al. 2010](https://doi.org/10.1108/10610421011033421)

- **F10.9 Spacing: opposite signs for text vs pictorial logos.** Gupta & Hagtvedt (2021) find spacious letter arrangement lowers safety perceptions and brand attitude; Sharma & Varki (2018) find added white space between elements of pictorial logos improves evaluation. — [Gupta & Hagtvedt](https://doi.org/10.1093/jcr/ucab006); [Sharma & Varki](https://doi.org/10.1080/00913367.2018.1463880)

- **F10.10 Counter-intuitive single-paper effects without replication found.** Rahinel & Nelson (2016): unstable logos increase the utility of safety products via inferences about the environment. Pittard et al. (2007): golden-ratio preference, limited set of ratios. Septianto & Paramita (2021): cuteness effect only under hope. Daryanto et al. (2022): one survey with a hypothetical logo. — [Rahinel & Nelson](https://doi.org/10.1093/jcr/ucw039); [Pittard et al.](https://doi.org/10.1108/02651330710761026); [Septianto & Paramita](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jretconser.2021.102734); [Daryanto et al.](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1057/s41262-022-00271-z)

- **F10.11 Data-driven alternative to hand-coded design features (pointer).** Dew, R., Ansari, A., & Toubia, O. (2022). Letting Logos Speak: Leveraging Multiview Representation Learning for Data-Driven Branding and Logo Design. *Marketing Science, 41*(2), 401–425. DOI 10.1287/mksc.2021.1326. Record verified (title, authors, journal, DOI); abstract not extracted. Relevant as a method for linking logo features to brand personality at scale. — [record](https://doi.org/10.1287/mksc.2021.1326)

### Inferences
- Summary evidence grading across this file (my synthesis):
  - Closest to "strong" (multiple independent teams, same direction): asymmetry ↔ excitement (F8.5); commitment/attachment-moderated resistance to substantial redesign (F9.1–F9.4); status-dependent logo prominence/size (F5.6, F5.7, F5.5).

- Three moderators recur across otherwise unrelated properties and deserve to be mandatory brief fields: (a) brand familiarity/stage (F1.3, F6.2, F7.6, F7.7, F9.x), (b) regulatory focus/perceived risk/product type — prevention-utilitarian-high-risk vs promotion-hedonic-low-risk (F2.5, F3.1, F4.1–F4.3), (c) positioning/personality — dynamic-modern-exciting vs stable-traditional (F2.1, F2.3, F2.6, F2.9, F8.5, F5.4).
